# Review: Verse of the Day Calendar (72 entries)

**Overall verdict:** This is unusually disciplined work. Most devotional calendars fail my review in the first ten entries on root fallacies, invented lexical claims, and promises detached from addressees. This one discloses textual variants (Philippians 4:13, Lamentations 3:22, Zephaniah 3:17), scopes covenant promises to their original recipients (Jeremiah 29:11, Isaiah 41:10, Isaiah 43:2, 2 Chronicles 7:14, Deuteronomy 31:8), and in several places explicitly declines the popular sermon point the verse is famous for. I verified the checkable claims below; the large majority hold. What follows is what doesn't — plus one genuine misuse-risk flag and two hedges I considered and am deliberately not raising.

## What earns real credit (verified, not just pleasant)

- **Philippians 4:13** — the manuscript claim is correct: the earliest witnesses (א*, A, B, D*) lack Χριστῷ; "Christ" is a later clarification. The "all things" scoping to the poverty-and-plenty context is exactly right. This is a model entry.
- **John 3:16** — the *monogenēs* treatment is textbook-correct, including the Hebrews 11:17 Isaac counterexample, and it refuses to overclaim against "only begotten" while still marking it as a theological rendering.
- **John 10:10** — explicitly refuses the *zōē*-as-special-lexical-category fallacy ("the claim rests on John's shepherd-and-salvation context rather than on a separate lexical 'kind' of life"). That sentence is the opposite of the usual error, and someone wrote it on purpose.
- **Lamentations 3:22** — correctly discloses the MT *tāmnû* ("we are not consumed") versus the emendation behind "never cease," and correctly marks the second colon as secure. Rare honesty for this verse.
- **Genesis 16:13** and **Isaiah 26:3** — both flag their own Hebrew as difficult and refuse to let the rendering carry more precision than the text supports ("should not bear more precision than that"; "'leans' should be treated as its picture, not as the lexical meaning").
- **Psalm 119:105** — declines the beloved two-scales-of-illumination sermon point ("the parallel may simply be poetic"). Correct: this is likely synonymous parallelism, and the entry says so.
- **1 Peter 5:7** — the *epiriptō* claim checks out (Luke 19:35 is indeed the only other NT occurrence), the participle-hanging-on-the-imperative grammar is right, and the pastoral caveat ("that does not make involuntary or recurring anxiety itself a sin of pride") closes off a real, common abuse of this text.
- **Hebrews 12:1** — I checked the root claim: *agōn* (12:1) does share its root with *katēgōnisanto* (11:33, "conquered kingdoms"). Obscure, correct, and actually load-bearing for the entry's point.
- **Mark 11:17** — *lēstēs* is in fact the word used for the men crucified beside Jesus (Mark 15:27). Correct.
- **Genesis 50:20** — the carry-it line "You don't have to [call the harm good]" pre-empts the standard misuse of this verse against abuse survivors. This is what risk-aware devotional writing looks like when it's done with a specific sentence rather than a vague disclaimer.

## Findings

### 1. Romans 8:28 — an undisclosed textual-and-syntactic decision, in a calendar whose own standard is disclosure

The translation reads: *"God works all things together for good."* That rendering takes a genuinely disputed position twice over: (a) some early witnesses (P46, A, B) include ὁ θεός as explicit subject, but the NA28 main text does not, and (b) even without the variant, whether the subject is "God," "all things," or "he/the Spirit" is a live syntactic dispute among specialists. The calendar discloses variants of comparable weight at Philippians 4:13, Lamentations 3:22, and Zephaniah 3:17 — so this isn't a complaint about a standard the calendar never adopted; it's an inconsistency in a standard it did adopt. The rendering is defensible. Presenting it silently is not, by this calendar's own rules.

### 2. Romans 5:8 — a verb tense asked to prove more than tense can prove

The noticeThis says: *"'Demonstrates' is present tense in the Greek, not past — Paul treats the cross, a past event, as God's standing demonstration of love: evidence still on display, not a fact filed away."* The present συνίστησιν is real, but a Greek present in a statement like this is naturally gnomic — the tense is *consistent with* "standing demonstration"; it does not *establish* it. The devotional point is fine and probably true of Paul's meaning. The problem is the framing, which presents an interpretive inference as a grammatical fact. Compare the calendar's own handling of the Hebrew perfects in Isaiah 41:10 ("their precise time-shading is debated") — that's the register this entry needed.

### 3. Psalm 145:18 — a flat "not" where the parallelism leaves the question open

The noticeThis asserts: *"the same act described twice, not two separate groups."* Hebrew parallelism here could be synonymous restatement, but it could equally be specification — the second colon genuinely qualifying the first (nearness belongs to those who call *in truth*, a narrower set than all callers). Many commentators take the qualifying reading. The entry's reading is respectable; stating "not two separate groups" as settled fact is an overclaim about what the poetic form can demonstrate. One word — "likely" or "most naturally" — fixes it.

### 4. Hosea 11:1 — a smoothed quotation of a textually difficult verse

The noticeThis quotes 11:2 as *"the more they were called, the more they went away."* The MT of 11:2 actually reads "they called to them, so they went from them" (third-person plural subject); the familiar "the more I called" wording follows the LXX or emendation. The paraphrase given is close to common English translations and the gist is not in dispute — but the entry presents it as what "the verse right after this one records," which is stronger than the MT supports. Given that this same entry is *about* how Matthew reuses Hosea, textual care here is not optional decoration.

### 5. Zephaniah 3:17 — an honest disclosure that undersells the problem

The entry discloses that the verb could mean "he will quiet you" or "he himself will fall silent," and says the Hebrew doesn't settle it. True — but the tradition is three-way, not two-way: the LXX reflects "he will renew you" (καινιεῖ, suggesting יְחַדֵּשׁ for יַחֲרִישׁ), and some modern translations follow that route. Disclosing two of three live options presents a narrower uncertainty than actually exists. This is the mildest kind of finding — an accurate disclosure that stops one step short — but the entry's whole devotional payoff ("there is a quiet in it too") rests on the two options it chose to mention.

### 6. Psalm 139:14 — internally wobbly root language, and one undisclosed variant

The noticeThis says *"the same Hebrew root appears twice in this verse... the forms here are related, not identical words."* "Same root" and "related, not identical" are pulling against each other — the underlying forms (from פלה/פלא, two closely allied roots that the tradition itself conflates) are precisely why the hedge exists, but as written the sentence asserts and retracts in the same breath. Separately, the first-person "I am wonderfully made" has a real ancient-versions alternative ("*You* are awesome[ly wonderful]," LXX/Syriac), undisclosed. Minor on both counts, but this entry is doing lexical work and should be crisp about it.

### 7. Hebrews 10:31 — a hedge so cautious it stops communicating

*"Related falling-language appears elsewhere for falling into a pit and, with a compound verb, for falling among robbers."* This is not wrong, but it's muddled: ἐμπίπτω — the actual verb in Hebrews 10:31 — is itself the compound, and it is the verb in both Luke 10:36 (fell among robbers) and Matthew 12:11 (sheep in a pit). The sentence reads like it was hedged past the point of saying what it means. The fix is to state the simple, checkable fact: the same verb appears in those two passages.

## Risk review

The instructions require me to distinguish genuine, specific misuse risks from generic hedges, and to say plainly which is which.

### One genuine flag — (a), specific sentence, specific misuse

**James 1:2, carryItWithYou:** *"Name one trial you're currently facing, and instead of asking 'how do I escape this,' ask 'what is this producing in me.'"*

The named sentence instructs a reader, about **any** unscoped current trial, to replace the escape-question with a formation-question. That is the exact rhetorical shape used in real coercive situations — "stop asking how to leave; ask what God is doing in you through this" — said to people in abusive marriages, exploitative churches, and dangerous households. The devotional doesn't intend that; but the sentence, lifted alone, performs it, because "instead of" makes escape and formation *rivals* rather than parallel questions. James's own letter doesn't support the rival framing — the same author thunders at oppressors and says the cries of the defrauded have reached the Lord's ears (James 5:1–6). The fix is one clause: scope "trial" to hardships that must be endured rather than harms that should be escaped, or drop "instead of" in favor of "alongside." Note that this calendar demonstrably knows how to do this — Genesis 50:20 and 1 Peter 5:7 both carry exactly the needed kind of guard sentence. Its absence here reads as an oversight, not a pattern.

### Two hedges considered and rejected — (b), named per instructions

- **Jonah 1:17** (*"if you're currently in a season that feels like confinement or setback, consider whether it might be God's means of preserving you"*): I considered flagging this as sacralizing confinement. It doesn't clear the bar. There is no directive against leaving, no sentence that makes endurance a test of faithfulness, and "consider whether" is an invitation, not a verdict. Any residual worry here is triggered by proximity to the word "confinement," not by a sentence a manipulator could actually deploy — which is precisely the generic hedge I'm instructed not to raise, so I'm not raising it.
- **Hebrews 10:31** (*"let this verse's fear stay a fear today"*): I considered a scrupulosity concern — telling an anxious reader to sit in dread. It doesn't clear the bar either, and the entry itself is why: it explicitly scopes the fear to the specific apostasy warning "not to ordinary doubt or distance." That scoping sentence is the actual protection an anxious reader needs, and it's already present. A vague caution stacked on top would say nothing true or specific.
