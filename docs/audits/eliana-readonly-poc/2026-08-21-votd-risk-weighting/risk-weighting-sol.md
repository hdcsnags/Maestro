The audience facts materially reduce the likelihood of some harms: the closed-test readers will probably have church relationships and are not encountering the app as completely isolated strangers. They do not eliminate the risks. ## James 1:2

> “Instead of asking ‘how do I escape this,’ ask ‘what is this producing in me.’”

### Likelihood

**Moderate.**

The explanation correctly distinguishes joy from pretending pain is enjoyable. But the final sentence creates an unnecessary opposition between escaping a trial and discerning what it may produce. For a normal churchgoing reader facing disappointment, illness, or vocational difficulty, it will probably function as intended. For someone facing abuse, exploitation, severe depression, or another preventable danger, “instead of asking how do I escape” can sound like a direct command not to seek safety.

That is not a remote hypothetical. Christians are regularly taught to interpret endurance as faithfulness, and this sentence could reinforce that pattern without anyone else present to identify the difference between persecution, ordinary hardship, and avoidable harm.

### Impact

**High in the vulnerable case; otherwise low.**

The common effect would be mild and potentially constructive: helping someone look for perseverance rather than immediate relief. The less common effect could be severe if it delays leaving danger, disclosing abuse, or obtaining treatment.

### Cost of the fix

**Almost none.**

James’s demanding claim about endurance does not require telling readers to stop asking whether escape is appropriate. The verse loses no force if discernment and safety are acknowledged.

A stronger application would be:

> “Name one trial you are facing. Without pretending the pain is good—or assuming you must remain in preventable danger—ask what faithful endurance could produce in you and what wise action the situation requires.”

### Recommendation

**Fix it.**

Keep the verse, the distinction between evaluation and emotion, and the call to endurance. Remove the false choice between escape and formation.

## Jonah 1:17

> “Consider whether it might be God’s means of preserving you for what comes next.”

### Likelihood

**Low to moderate during the closed test; moderate in a wider release.**

Most readers will probably receive this as hope that an unwanted setback is not necessarily abandonment. That is pastorally useful. The risk comes from applying Jonah’s specifically interpreted event to an undefined “season of confinement.” A reader in coercive control, an unsafe home, punitive church discipline, or severe isolation could interpret the confinement itself as divinely assigned and therefore something not to resist.

Because the app has no way to know what “confinement” means in the reader’s life, the application asks for a providential conclusion the reader is not equipped to establish from this verse alone.

### Impact

**Potentially high.**

If applied to ordinary frustration, little harm follows. If applied to abuse or coercive confinement, attributing the situation to God’s preserving action could delay help and add spiritual guilt to any attempt to leave.

### Cost of the fix

**Low.**

The theological force lies in God’s unexpected rescue of Jonah, not in teaching that present confinement should ordinarily be interpreted as divine protection.

A safer version would be:

> “If a setback has left you feeling enclosed or abandoned, remember that God can preserve and redirect a life through means no one expected. Do not assume every confinement comes from Him; ask for discernment, and seek safe counsel where harm or coercion is involved.”

For a shorter card:

> “A dark or unexpected circumstance is not proof that God has abandoned you. Ask how He may preserve and redirect you through it, without assuming the circumstance itself is good.”

### Recommendation

**Fix it.**

The current sentence crosses from hope in providence to speculation about the divine purpose of the reader’s particular confinement.

## Genesis 50:20

### Likelihood

**Moderate.**

Joseph speaks after the good outcome has become historically visible: people have been preserved alive. ### Impact

**Moderate to high.**

The likely harm is self-blame or manufactured testimony: “If I cannot identify God’s intention, I must be bitter, faithless, or insufficiently healed.” In more severe cases, the language can be used to minimize abuse or protect the offender from full accountability.

**Very low, and the fix improves the exegesis.**

> “Think of a real harm you cannot call good. You do not have to relabel it or identify a hidden benefit. Ask for grace to trust that human evil does not place your life beyond God’s redeeming work—even where you cannot yet see the outcome.”

### Recommendation

**Fix the application; keep the exposition.**

The central treatment is unusually responsible. The final sentence simply asks for more interpretive certainty than many harmed readers honestly possess.

**Moderate, occasionally high.**

### Cost of the fix

**Fix it lightly.**

## Matthew 6:33

> “The promise is that the necessities in view—food, drink, clothing—will be added by that same Father as his people give first place to his kingdom and righteousness.”

### Likelihood

**Moderate.**

The entry correctly limits “all these things” to necessities rather than prosperity, and “his people” is better than turning the promise into an individual wealth formula. Nevertheless, readers may still hear a transactional guarantee: faithful kingdom-seeking believers will not lack food, clothing, or shelter.

That conclusion collides with both Christian experience and the New Testament’s own witness to hunger, exposure, persecution, and martyrdom. A poor or food-insecure believer may infer that continued deprivation exposes deficient faith or disordered priorities.

The application itself is comparatively safe. It says to spend a few minutes seeking the kingdom and then act; it does not tell the reader to neglect the practical concern.

### Impact

**Moderate.**

The most realistic harm is spiritual shame or confusion when basic needs remain unmet. In extreme poverty, the promise could be used to minimize material need or substitute exhortation for concrete congregational assistance.

### Cost of the fix

**A genuine but manageable theological cost.**

This is the closest call among the eight. Overqualification can make Jesus sound as though he merely offered a general spiritual encouragement rather than a real promise concerning the Father’s care. The answer is not to evacuate the promise but to state what it is not: a mechanical guarantee that no faithful individual will experience deprivation.

A careful revision:

> “Jesus gives a real promise of the Father’s knowledge and care for His people, not a prosperity formula or a verdict that anyone who lacks necessities has failed to seek the kingdom. His kingdom also teaches His people to become part of the Father’s provision for one another.”

That last sentence preserves force and places responsibility on the community rather than on the hungry individual alone.

### Recommendation

**Fix it, but narrowly.**

Keep the command, the material meaning of “these things,” and the assurance of the Father’s care. Add one sentence preventing the promise from becoming either a formula or an accusation against the poor.

# Does the calendar still disciple with depth?

**Yes. As a whole, this is substantive discipleship material, not neutralized inspirational content.** It repeatedly refuses popular simplifications:

- Philippians 4:13 is about steadfastness in poverty and plenty, not limitless achievement.
- Hebrews 10:31 is allowed to remain genuinely fearful.
- Psalm 88 is permitted to end in darkness.
- Habakkuk 3:17 names loss before moving to rejoicing.
- Romans 8:28 defines “good” through conformity to Christ rather than improved circumstances.
- Isaiah 58:6 demands concrete action against oppression.
- Jeremiah 7:11 confronts religious practice used as cover for wrongdoing.
- John 15:5 retains the absolute claim that apart from Christ believers can do nothing.

Those are not cautious, generic sentiments. They challenge comfortable churchgoers, correct familiar misreadings, and give suffering readers comfort without consistently promising quick relief.

The proposed safety changes above would not flatten the calendar because they do not qualify the biblical assertions themselves. They correct unwarranted jumps from a passage to the reader’s unknown circumstance:

- James can command endurance without commanding someone to remain in preventable danger.
- Paul can celebrate power in weakness without making treatment faithless.
- Joseph can confess providence without requiring every survivor to identify a visible good.
- Jesus can promise the Father’s care without turning deprivation into evidence of failed discipleship.
- Christian patience and loyal love can remain costly without granting unsafe people continued access.

That is not theology weakened by therapeutic caution. It is more exact moral and pastoral application.

There are, however, places elsewhere in the calendar where caution or technical precision has begun to cost devotional force. The clearest examples are:

> “Ask for His support in your own calling without treating Israel’s oracle as though it had originally been addressed to you by name.”  
> — Isaiah 41:10

> “Ask God for the presence and steadiness needed for your own obedience rather than claiming Joshua’s commission unchanged as a personal guarantee.”  
> — Deuteronomy 31:8

> “Take that step without turning the lamp-and-path distinction into a precision the Hebrew may not intend.”  
> — Psalm 119:105

These qualifications are intellectually responsible, but the final line of a devotional card is not always the best place for them. The Psalm 119 sentence in particular leaves the reader thinking about an interpretive disclaimer rather than walking by the light of God’s word. The Isaiah and Deuteronomy qualifications could be stated positively: the original promise belongs to Israel or Joshua, and it reveals the character of the God Christians trust in their own calling. That would preserve contextual integrity without ending at arm’s length from the text.

Similarly, observations about textual difficulty, manuscript history, and lexical limits are often excellent study notes, but not all of them need to govern the final pastoral movement. A committed Christian reader needs careful interpretation, but also needs the text to arrive as summons, warning, consolation, and promise. This calendar usually accomplishes that. Its larger danger is not theological timidity; it is that a handful of applications universalize a biblical character’s interpreted circumstance before knowing the reader’s circumstance.
