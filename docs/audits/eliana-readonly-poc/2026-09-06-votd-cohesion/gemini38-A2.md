#### Entry 27: John 3:16
- **Verdicts:** `INSIGHT_SUSPECT`, `FILLER`, `DRIFT`
- **Insight Issue:** The insight dogmatically claims that deriving *monogenēs* from begetting is a "root-fallacy" and asserts the suffix comes from *genos* ("kind/class"), not *gennaō*. In historical Greek linguistics, compound adjectives in -γενής derive from the PIE root *gen-* (which underlies both generation/birth and family/kind). While semantic usage in Luke and Hebrews 11:17 establishes the meaning "unique / one-of-a-kind," calling "only begotten" an etymological root fallacy based on *genos* is itself a discredited lexical trope.
- **Card Issue:** The card absorbs this linguistic tension and spends Notice This defending modern translations against the KJV and discussing Abraham and Ishmael in Hebrews 11:17. This is filler. Meanwhile, the insight had a brilliant grammatical observation that directly anchors Why It Matters: *houtōs... hōste* signifies manner ("in this way"), showing *how* God loved rather than merely measuring volume. Notice This should highlight that manner.
- **Ranked Insight Correction:**
  1. *Primary:* Ground "one and only / unique" in contextual semantics (BDAG: unique in relationship) without asserting a false etymological dichotomy between *genos* and *gennaō*.
  2. *Secondary:* Emphasize the *houtōs... hōste* syntax: the gift of the Son demonstrates the *manner* and cost of divine love, not just its emotional volume.
- **Card Rewrite:**
  - **Notice This:** “In this way” describes how God loved, not merely how much: the cross is the concrete shape of His love, not just a measurement of its volume. Calling Jesus His “one and only” Son marks Him as utterly unique — the irreplaceable gift given for the world.

  2. *Secondary:* Note transparently that ancient versions read 3cp *tammū* ("his mercies never cease"), matching line 2, without conflating the subjects in the exposition.
- **Card Rewrite:**

#### Entry 35: Psalms 139:14
- **Verdicts:** `INSIGHT_SUSPECT`, `FILLER`
- **Insight Issue:** The insight asserts: *"The same root, פ־ל־א (pala), appears twice in this verse — first as נִפְלֵיתִי... then again as נִפְלָאִים..."* This is morphologically incorrect. In the Masoretic Text (WLC), `נִפְלֵיתִי` is pointed as a III-he/yod form from פָּלָה (*pālāh*, Strong's 6395, "to separate, distinguish, set apart"; cf. Ps 4:4, 17:7). `נִפְלָאִים` is from the III-aleph root פָּלָא (*pālā’*, Strong's 6381, "to be wonderful, extraordinary"). While closely related and phonetically paired, they are distinct roots in Hebrew lexicography.
- **Card Issue:** The card attempts to patch this by hedging (*"That root is also used elsewhere... the forms here are related, not identical words"*), wasting word count on pedantic qualifications that offer zero spiritual sustenance.
- **Ranked Insight Correction:**
  1. *Primary:* Recognize `נִפְלֵיתִי` as *pālāh* ("wonderfully set apart / distinguished"), highlighting God's personal, deliberate craftsmanship of the individual.
  2. *Secondary:* Note the poetic wordplay between personal distinction (*pālāh*) and God's awe-inspiring cosmic wonders (*pālā’*).
- **Card Rewrite:**
  - **Notice This:** The psalmist pairs two closely linked Hebrew words: he praises God for being “wonderfully set apart” (*nifleiti*), and marvels that God’s works are “wondrous” (*nifla'im*). Your own life is crafted with the same intentional wonder God displays across creation.
