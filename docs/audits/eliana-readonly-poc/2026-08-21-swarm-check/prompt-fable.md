# The Forsaken Cry

## ʾĒlî ʾĒlî lĕmā šĕbaqtānî — Mark 15:34 · Matthew 27:46 · Psalm 22

*Council Edition — prepared for the full council (Fable 5, Sol, Opus, and friends)*


## 1. The Scene and the Anomaly

### 1.1 Six hours, one sentence

**[direct quotation]** From the third hour to the ninth, a man hangs dying, and Mark gives us almost nothing of what he said. The scope deserves to be stated precisely, because it is easy to overstate. Within Mark's crucifixion scene proper — from the moment they crucify him (15:24) to the moment he breathes his last (15:37) — Jesus speaks exactly once, at 15:34.[^1^] The curt Σὺ λέγεις ("You say so," 15:2) belongs to the trial before Pilate, before any nail. And the loud voice of 15:37 — ἀφεὶς φωνὴν μεγάλην ἐξέπνευσεν, "he released a great voice and breathed his last" — carries no words at all.[^2^] Between the nails and the last breath: one sentence.

Two features of that sentence deserve to slow us down before we go anywhere else.

The first is that **the sound was kept**. Mark does not merely report what Jesus said; he spells the words out in Greek letters — ελωι ελωι λεμα σαβαχθανι — and then translates them himself: ὁ θεός μου ὁ θεός μου, εἰς τί ἐγκατέλιπές με;[^3^] Mark does this rarely. *Talitha koum* at a girl's bedside (5:41). *Ephphatha* over a deaf man's ears (7:34). *Abba* in the garden (14:36). Each time the pattern is the same: transliterate, then translate. When a writer keeps the sound and then explains it, he is telling you *this is what he actually said*, in the language he actually said it in, and he does not want you to lose the sound of it.

The second is the volume. Both Mark and Matthew say he cried out φωνῇ μεγάλῃ (phōnē megalē) — with a great voice.[^2^] The physiology should be handled honestly. The once-standard account of crucifixion as death by suffocation is now contested: Maslen and Mitchell showed that the medical literature largely recycles earlier medical papers rather than the ancient sources,[^4^] and suspension experiments found that volunteers with outstretched arms breathed without difficulty — which is why the physical anthropologist Joe Zias calls the classic asphyxiation model "no longer tenable if the arms are outstretched."[^5^] The most-cited medical study reads the sequence cry-then-death as suggesting "a catastrophic terminal event" — a hypothesis, not a verdict.[^6^] [shared theme] What survives every model is smaller and stronger: speech costs breath, breath late in crucifixion is scarce, and a shout spends what little remains. This was not a groan that escaped him. He spent something to say this.

### 1.2 Four accounts side by side

**[direct quotation]** Before we interpret a single word, put all four Gospels on the table.

| Gospel | Words recorded | Original tongue kept? | Source text | Loud voice? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 15:34 | ελωι ελωι λεμα σαβαχθανι — with Mark's own translation: ὁ θεός μου ὁ θεός μου, εἰς τί ἐγκατέλιπές με; | Yes — Semitic sound spelled in Greek letters, then translated | Psalm 22:1 (Eng.) / 22:2 (MT) | Yes — here (φωνῇ μεγάλῃ), and again at 15:37, a great voice with no words |
| Matthew 27:46 | ηλει ηλει λεμα σαβαχθανι (Byzantine witnesses read ηλι) — translated: θεέ μου θεέ μου, ἵνατί με ἐγκατέλιπες;[^7^] | Yes — same pattern: transcription, then translation | Psalm 22:1 (Eng.) / 22:2 (MT) | Yes — here, and again at 27:50 (πάλιν κράξας φωνῇ μεγάλῃ), wordless[^8^] |
| Luke 23:46 | Πάτερ, εἰς χεῖράς σου παρατίθεμαι τὸ πνεῦμά μου — "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit" | No | Psalm 31:5 (Eng.) / 31:6 (MT)[^9^] | Yes — one loud cry, and Luke gives it these words |
| John 19:30 | Τετέλεσται — one word, perfect tense: "it stands completed" | No | No psalm quoted | **No** — John writes only εἶπεν, "he said"[^8^] |

Read the last column twice, because the first edition of this study got it wrong. It claimed that all four Gospels record a loud voice at the end. That is false for John, and this edition corrects it: John 19:30 has no loud-voice note of any kind — ὅτε οὖν ἔλαβεν τὸ ὄξος ὁ Ἰησοῦς εἶπεν Τετέλεσται, καὶ κλίνας τὴν κεφαλὴν παρέδωκεν τὸ πνεῦμα. He said it; he bowed his head; he handed over the spirit.[^8^] The loud voice belongs to the three Synoptics: twice in Mark (15:34, 37), twice in Matthew (27:46, 50), once in Luke (23:46).

**[textual evidence]** And a second fact emerges only when the four are laid side by side. In Mark and Matthew, the *final* loud cry — the one at the moment of death — is contentless. A great voice, and nothing quoted. Only Luke assigns the last loud cry any words, and the words he assigns are a different psalm entirely.

Three of the four accounts, then, put a psalm in his mouth at the end. Mark and Matthew give him the opening line of Psalm 22; Luke gives him Psalm 31:5 (Eng.) / 31:6 (MT). John gives him no psalm and no question — one word, Τετέλεσται.

Note, finally, the difference in the first two rows — display it now; do not analyze it yet. Mark's ελωι spells an Aramaic sound (ʾĕlāhî); Matthew's ηλει the Hebrew one (ʾēlî). The rest of the cry is letter-for-letter identical in both. One cry, kept in two tongues. That seam is where the next chapter begins.

Four accounts. One death. **Three of them end with a psalm on his lips, and one of those psalms is a question.** Which raises the question this entire study exists to answer. Out of everything a man could say with the last strength he has — *why this?*

## 2. One Cry, Two Languages

Chapter 1 set the four accounts side by side. Now the spade goes in. Mark and Matthew alone preserve the cry as a sound — a phonetic transcription with a translation attached — and the two transcriptions do not match. That difference is not a problem to be harmonized. It is the evidence.

### 2.1 The two forms

**[lexical evidence]** Psalm 22:1 (Eng.) / 22:2 (MT) reads אֵלִי אֵלִי לָמָּה עֲזַבְתָּנִי — ʾēlî ʾēlî lāmâ ʿăzabtānî, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me." Set the two gospel forms beside it:

| Element | Mark 15:34 (NA28) | Matthew 27:46 (NA28) | Underlying form | Parsing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Address | ελωι ελωι (elōi) | ηλει ηλει (ēlei; Byzantine ηλι) | Mark: Aramaic אֱלָהִי ʾĕlāhî; Matthew: Hebrew אֵלִי ʾēlî | noun "God" + 1cs suffix, "my God" |
| "Why" | λεμα (lemā) | λεμα (lemā) | Aramaic לְמָה, not MT's לָמָּה | lĕ + mâ, "for what?" |
| Verb | σαβαχθανι | σαβαχθανι | Aramaic שְׁבַקְתַּנִי (šĕbaqtānî), root שבק, not MT's עֲזַבְתָּנִי | Peal perfect 2ms + 1cs object, "you have forsaken me" |

The standard lexical verdict: Mark's ελωι transcribes the Aramaic form of "my God"; Matthew's ηλει transcribes the Hebrew.[^10^] And σαβαχθανι is Aramaic in **both** — Thayer notes it stands in the gospels where the psalm's Hebrew has ʿăzabtānî, "which is so rendered also by the Chaldee paraphrast."[^11^] So Mark's line is fully Aramaic; Matthew's is mixed — a Hebrew address riding an Aramaic verb. France reads the evidence the same way, while flagging that even Mark's Aramaic is not the extant Targum's wording.[^12^] Two complications deserve naming, not burying: Dalman thought Mark's ελωι could itself represent a Hebrew אֱלֹהַי, and P. J. Williams notes that ēlî-type forms are abundantly attested in Aramaic too, so Matthew's line is not provably "mixed."[^13^] The majority description stands; it is not unanimous.

**[contested text]** Neither writer standardized the sound, and the manuscripts show what that cost. The apparatus of Mark 15:34 is a variant cloud: ελωει in some witnesses, ηλει in Codex Bezae (D); λαμα, λιμα, λειμα beside λεμα; and more than a dozen spellings of the σαβαχθανι-word across the tradition — σαβακτανει, ζαβαφθανει, σιβακθανει, even the garbled σαβαχθαχθανι.[^14^] The harmonizing pressure ran both ways: Vaticanus and Sinaiticus pull Matthew toward Mark's ελωι, while Bezae pulls Mark toward Matthew, reading ηλει ηλει λαμα ζαφθανει in both gospels — and, in Mark only, substituting εἰς τί ὠνείδισάς με, "why have you reproached me," a reading for which D is the sole Greek witness, with Old Latin support; the ECM of Mark (2021) treats it as a secondary correction.[^15^] The tradition had every scribal incentive to normalize this utterance, and largely did not. Remember why the sound matters: the bystanders hear "Elijah," and that confusion is phonetically easier from Matthew's ēli than from Mark's elōi — yet Mark kept his harder form anyway. The transmission history corroborates the claim this study is built on: **the sound was too precious to standardize.**

### 2.2 The vernacular argument runs on Mark

**[theological synthesis]** Here is the authenticity argument in its strongest form. A legend-maker composing a death scene to prove scriptural fulfillment would have put the psalm's scriptural Hebrew in Jesus' mouth — ʾēlî ʾēlî lāmâ ʿăzabtānî, the text of the scroll. What the tradition actually preserves is the psalm vernacularized: the address in kitchen Aramaic, the verb šbq where the scroll has ʿzb. And the rest of the tradition behaves exactly as embarrassment predicts. Luke replaces the cry altogether with the composed serenity of Psalm 31:5, "Father, into your hands"; John omits it; the Gospel of Peter rewrites it as "My power, my power, you have forsaken me" — a softening whose docetic reading is now disputed, but whose evasion of "my God" is plain.[^16^] Two canonical evangelists and one apocryphon all flinch from the same sentence. People do not flinch from things they invented.

**And this is exactly why the argument has to run on Mark.** Mark's line is fully vernacular; Matthew's has the Hebrew address restored. Which direction did the change run? Hagner, assuming Markan priority, thinks Matthew Hebraized the address, "perhaps with an eye on the confusion with the name 'Elijah' in v. 47."[^17^] Randall Buth argues the reverse: Matthew's Hebrew ηλι preserves the actual utterance, and Mark deliberately recast it in Aramaic for a literary function within his narrative pattern of transliterations.[^18^] Dalman and Williams allow that a bilingual tradition could have carried both forms from the start. **[open question]** The priority of the two forms is genuinely unresolved, and this study does not resolve it. What can be said: both languages are defensible as Jesus' prayer language. The old consensus that Hebrew was a dead liturgical language has been broken by Grintz, Safrai, Buth, and Notley, who make a serious case that Mishnaic Hebrew was spoken alongside Aramaic; Casey's reconstructions represent the classic Aramaic-primacy position.[^18^] Either Matthew preserves an older memory or a later polish toward the psalm — but in either case it is Mark, not Matthew, who preserves the untidied sound. Work from Matthew alone and you will make the authenticity argument on the one verse that partly undercuts it.

### 2.3 Not a lift from the Greek Bible

**[lexical evidence]** The Septuagint's version of this verse reads ὁ θεὸς ὁ θεός μου, πρόσχες μοι· ἵνα τί ἐγκατέλιπές με — "O God, my God, attend to me; why have you forsaken me?" (LXX Ps 21:2 = MT 22:2 = Eng. 22:1; both the Greek and the Hebrew count the superscription as verse 1).[^19^] The phrase πρόσχες μοι, "attend to me," stands in the reconstructed Old Greek text with no apparatus note of omission, and it has no counterpart in any Hebrew manuscript, in the Targum, or in the Peshitta.[^20^] It appears in neither gospel. Mark reads εἰς τί ἐγκατέλιπές με; Matthew, ἵνατί με ἐγκατέλιπες. Whoever translated the cry into Greek omitted the LXX's own expansion — and a copyist lifting the verse from the Greek Bible would have had no reason to cut the Greek Bible's words.

An earlier draft of this study claimed the Septuagint evidence "points the wrong way," as though it weakened the case. The correction runs in the opposite direction and is stronger. Martin Karrer states the conclusion plainly: "His followers translated the quotation into the Greek in their own right and in different ways. They did not rely on the Greek text of Psalm 22."[^21^] France and Gundry concur in substance, and Karrer adds a manuscript observation worth keeping: the great codices never assimilate the LXX psalm text to the gospel citations or the citations to the psalm — the two textual streams stayed independent on the page.[^22^] **[strong allusion]** What the evangelists translated, then, was not a Greek text but a Semitic utterance.

One more witness confirms which Semitic utterance. The Targum of this very verse reads אלי אלי מטול מה שבקתני — ʾēlî ʾēlî miṭṭûl mâ šĕbaqtānî; the Peshitta Psalter reads ܐܠܗܝ ܐܠܗܝ ܠܡܢܐ ܫܒܩܬܢܝ — ʾalāhî ʾalāhî lĕmānā šbaqtānî, verbatim identical to the clause the Peshitta prints at Mark 15:34.[^23^] Both Aramaic renderings of Psalm 22 reach for the same verb, šbq, that sounds in the cry from the cross. **Jesus prays the psalm the way it lived in the vernacular** — not reciting the Hebrew of the scroll, but praying the translation his world prayed. The gospels kept the sound; the versions kept the idiom; the Greek Bible kept its distance. All three facts point the same way.

## 3. What the Crowd Heard

The cry did not fall into silence. It fell among listeners, and the gospels record what the listeners made of it. This is the only contemporaneous reception the cry ever received, and it is a mishearing. That fact deserves slow attention rather than embarrassment.

### 3.1 They misheard him

**[direct quotation]** Assign the speakers accurately, because the two accounts differ and both deserve to be quoted as they stand. In Mark 15:35 it is τινες τῶν παρεστηκότων — "some of the bystanders" — who say ἴδε Ἠλίαν φωνεῖ, "See, he is calling Elijah." In Mark 15:36 the "wait" line belongs to the sponge-bearer himself: δραμὼν δέ τις … λέγων, ἄφετε, ἴδωμεν εἰ ἔρχεται Ἠλίας καθελεῖν αὐτόν — "Wait, let us see whether Elijah comes to take him down."[^24^] The centurion says nothing here; he speaks at 15:39. Matthew distributes the lines across three groups: τινες make the identification (27:47), εἷς ἐξ αὐτῶν runs with the sour wine (27:48), and οἱ λοιποί — "the rest" — say ἄφες, ἴδωμεν εἰ ἔρχεται Ἠλίας σώσων αὐτόν, "whether Elijah comes to save him" (27:49).[^25^] Mark's scene is compressed, Matthew's choreographed; neither is a copy of the other at the level of who speaks.

**[contested text]** One manuscript note belongs here because it sits in this very verse. After Matthew 27:49, an ancient group of witnesses — ℵ B C L among them — adds ἄλλος δὲ λαβὼν λόγχην ἔνυξεν αὐτοῦ τὴν πλευράν, καὶ ἐξῆλθεν ὕδωρ καὶ αἷμα, "and another, taking a spear, pierced his side, and water and blood came out," placing a piercing before the death in reversed fluid order against John 19:34. Despite its elite attestation, the editorial consensus judges it a harmonization to John.[^25^] It is real, it is early, and it is secondary. The spear belongs to another chain; set it down openly and move on.

**[shared theme]** Why Elijah at all? The expectation was old and documented. Malachi 4:5 (Eng.) / 3:23 (MT) — הִנֵּה־אָנֹכִי שֹׁלֵחַ לָכֶם אֵת אֵלִיָּה הַנָּבִיא, hinnê-ʾānōkî šōlēaḥ lākem ʾēt ʾēliyyâ hannābîʾ, "Behold, I am sending you Elijah the prophet before the great and terrible day of the LORD comes" — and Sirach 48:10, written around 200 BC, already builds on it.[^26^] Mishnah Eduyot 8:7 records the Sages' version of his task: he comes "neither to put away nor to bring near, but to make peace in the world."[^27^] Now the discipline. The Mishnah's Elijah is an adjudicator and herald — not a last-moment rescuer of dying men. Commentaries everywhere assert the popular belief that Elijah would rescue the righteous in extremity; one of them states the awkward truth plainly: "There is no biblical basis for this idea."[^28^] The explicit sources for the rescuer folklore are later rabbinic. The earliest attestation of the belief may be this scene itself. That does not make the belief false — something made a Jerusalem crowd reach for Elijah in a heartbeat — but it makes it an inference, and it is labeled one here.

### 3.2 The crux, left open

**[open question]** Here is the puzzle, and it only appears once you stop working from Matthew alone. In Matthew the confusion is easy to hear: ηλει — ʾēlî — sits two syllables from ʾēliyyāhû, and "my God" is literally the front half of Elijah's name. In Mark it is hard: ελωι — ʾĕlāhî — does not resemble Ἠλίας nearly as well. **And Mark reports the same confusion anyway.**[^29^] Honest scholarship goes three directions, and each direction has named defenders:

| Option | The claim | Defenders |
|---|---|---|
| Matthew Hebraized | Mark's Aramaic is prior; Matthew altered the address toward the Hebrew of the psalm, "perhaps with an eye on the confusion with the name 'Elijah' in v. 47" | Hagner[^17^] |
| Mark Aramaized | The Hebrew-sounding form is primitive — "the reaction of the bystanders … is more intelligible if the cry was uttered in Hebrew"; Mark deliberately recast it in Aramaic for a literary function in his narrative | Lagrange, Taylor; Buth[^30^][^18^] |
| Never phonetic | The mishearing was theological before it was acoustic: any righteous man's desperate cry to God could be heard as a summons of the expected rescuer | SIL notes; Smith; Strauss[^31^] |

Randall Buth's 2014 study — "The Riddle of Jesus' Cry from the Cross" — is the one specialist treatment of exactly this riddle, and its title frames the choice: Matthew's form carries *the meaning*, Mark's carries *a literary function*.[^18^] This chapter does not adjudicate. The pun is obvious only in Matthew; the audit was right to reopen it, and here it stays open.

But notice what the crux itself establishes. The two gospels transcribe different sounds and report the identical mishearing — and the mishearing is phonetically *easier* from Matthew's form, yet Mark kept his harder form rather than smooth the scene toward sense. Centuries later Codex Bezae did the smoothing for him, reading ηλει ηλει in both gospels.[^29^] The transmission had every incentive to normalize the sound and largely refused. **The confusion is evidence that what reached the crowd was a sound, not a citation** — loud enough to be heard across an execution ground, distorted enough through a dying man's throat to be misheard.

And there is a sharper edge under the historical one. The crowd heard the first two syllables of the cry and reached for a prophet who did not come. Much of the church since has heard the first three words of the psalm and reached for a severance the text never says. **Both are hearing the first three words and stopping.** The crowd had an excuse. They were standing in the noise.

## 4. The Method Question: Quoting a First Line

Chapter 3 ended at an impasse. One reading hears the cry as plain despair and stops there; another hears the first line of a psalm whose ending is triumph, and so hears coded victory in the mouth of the dying. Between them stands a methodological claim that must be tried before it can be used — *quoting a psalm's first line invokes the whole psalm*. This chapter tries the claim: the practice is ancient, the label is not, the strong version fails — and what survives is narrower and stronger.

### 4.1 The practice is older than the label

**[lexical evidence — the practice]** The kernel is real. In the Mishnah, the Levites' daily Temple psalms are identified by their opening words, not by number: "On the first day of the week they would recite the psalm beginning: 'A psalm of David. The earth is the Lord's and all it contains…'" — and so through the week.[^32^] Nor was this a rabbinic peculiarity. Referring to a psalm by its chapter number "was almost non-extant in Jewish discourse until the 16th century"; psalms were named by their first line or by their content.[^33^] In Jesus' world, אֵלִי אֵלִי (ʾēlî ʾēlî) simply *was* how you said "Psalm 22." And there is one genuinely whole-psalm rabbinic move with this very psalm: in b. Megillah 15b, Esther begins her prayer with Psalm 22:1 (Eng.) / 22:2 (MT) and continues straight into verses 20–21 (Eng.) / 21–22 (MT).[^34^]

**[contested text — the label]** But the word usually attached to this practice must be surrendered. *Remez* (רֶמֶז, "hint") is one of the four senses of the medieval PaRDeS scheme — peshat, remez, derash, sod — and the acronym itself is thirteenth-century.[^35^] It names a level of meaning, not a citation technique. No rabbinic text names a rule "quote the first verse to invoke the whole"; one researcher who went looking reported exactly that: "I could find no substantiation for such a practice in Judaism during the time of Christ."[^36^] The rabbinic default is atomistic — exegesis of a verse or two at a time — which cuts against any mechanical incipit rule.[^33^] Even the Esther parallel carries a warning: Berkovitz has shown that the rabbinic whole-psalm reading of Psalm 22 crystallized in late antiquity partly as a counter-narrative to Christian claims on the psalm.[^37^] It is reception, not background. **The practice is ancient; the label is not; and the rule, as a rule, does not exist.**

### 4.2 The strong version fails

The strong apologetic version says: he was not really forsaken; he was signalling the psalm's happy ending. Dismantle it before anyone leans on it. Three problems.

First, Mark supplies a translation. If the point were *recall this psalm*, a citation formula would serve far better than a phonetic transcription plus a gloss. The transcription foregrounds the sound of an utterance, not the text of a scroll — and Mark, who explains Jewish customs elsewhere for Gentile readers, explains nothing here about an incipit convention.[^38^] Second, the psalm does not resolve until its hinge. The lament's וְלֹא־תַעֲנֶה (wĕlōʾ taʿăneh, "you do not answer," Psalm 22:2 Eng. / 22:3 MT) stands open for twenty verses until עֲנִיתָנִי (ʿănîtānî, "you answered me," Psalm 22:21 Eng. / 22:22 MT). Invoking the whole psalm *includes* the unanswered stretch; it does not cancel it. Third, the bystanders heard a cry. Their Elijah confusion tells you how the utterance registered: as an appeal, not a pointer.

The guild's resistance is on record. France: reading the whole psalm into Jesus' few words undermines "the effect that Mark intended, which was one of extreme agony." Gundry calls the despairing opening a "singularly inapt pointer" to the psalm's later confidence; Moo's monograph on the Old Testament in the passion narratives resists the inference at length.[^39^] Brown warns the move risks "almost the opposite meaning of what Jesus is portrayed as saying"; Hooker finds no evidence Mark had the rest of the psalm in mind here; Juel calls the triumphal reading "almost comical."[^40^] And Foster, himself sympathetic to the study of scriptural echoes, presses the question that has no good answer for the strong version: if Mark wished to depict future vindication, why did he not cite the part of the psalm that speaks of vindication?[^41^]

### 4.3 What survives

**[theological synthesis]** What survives is the conditional form, and it has a proper scholarly name — not *remez* but *metalepsis*: Hays's term for an allusion that "evokes resonances of the earlier text beyond those explicitly cited."[^42^] Hays applies it directly here: "Mark has signalled his readers that the whole psalm is to be read as a prefiguration of Jesus' destiny."[^43^] The decisive refinement is Ahearne-Kroll's. In the Greek psalm Mark knew, the praise of the second half is a *promise conditional on deliverance* — not an accomplished vindication smuggled back into the lament.[^44^] Read whole, the psalm intensifies the cry; it does not reverse it. A recent study of the lament psalms and Jesus' death weighs the options and lands in the same place: the vindication half is conditional on the deliverance pleaded for in the lament half, so the whole psalm "can be interpreted alongside Jesus' cry without reversing its meaning."[^45^] Holly Carey's monograph — the fullest case that Mark cites the psalm contextually — insists on the same asymmetry: the whole-psalm reading "need not subtract from the sense of Jesus' distress, but rather can enhance it," because the psalm's Righteous Sufferer suffers acutely and at length.[^46^]

**[strong allusion]** And notice where the strongest warrant lies. Not in a citation convention — in the evangelists' demonstrated practice. Mark has already woven the psalm's middle into his narrative before Jesus opens his mouth: the divided garments (Psalm 22:18 Eng. / 22:19 MT → Mark 15:24), the wagging heads and the mockers' taunt (Psalm 22:7–8 Eng. / 22:8–9 MT → Mark 15:29–32). Combs observes that the allusions run in reverse order — 18, then 7–8, then 1 — as if the chapter were walking backward up the psalm toward its first line.[^47^] Within a generation, Hebrews puts the psalm's resolution verse on the Messiah's own lips: "I will tell of your name to my brothers; in the midst of the congregation I will sing your praise" (Psalm 22:22 Eng. / 22:23 MT → Hebrews 2:12).[^48^] The whole psalm was in play — for the evangelists, demonstrably; for the historical Jesus, it remains beyond demonstration, and this study does not pretend otherwise.

**The version that survives is not "not forsaken, actually triumphant." It is "genuinely forsaken — and the psalm names where this ends."** First, though, chapter 5 puts the load-bearing word — *forsaken* — under the lens, because everything just claimed stands or falls on what that word can and cannot mean. Then chapter 6 reads the psalm itself to see how it ends; chapter 8 takes up the writer to the Hebrews, who heard its ending in Jesus' own voice.

## 5. The Word He Did Not Use

Chapter 4 left one sentence standing — *genuinely forsaken, and the psalm names where this ends* — and that sentence leans entirely on one verb. Chapter 2 established what the cry sounded like and whose idiom it spoke. Now a narrower question with a sharper edge: when the first translators carried the cry across languages, what did they do with its most dangerous word? The answer is the strongest single piece of evidence in this study — a piece of evidence about a choice somebody declined to make.

### 5.1 šĕbaq's double range — and its exact Greek mirror

**[lexical evidence]** Aramaic šĕbaq is a wide word. The Comprehensive Aramaic Lexicon runs its senses in order: *to leave, depart from; to abandon, leave alone; to permit, allow* — and then, natively and lexically, *to condone, to forgive*, including *to forgive a debt*.[^49^] It is the ordinary verb of the Lord's Prayer in Syriac: ܘܫܒܘܩ ܠܢ ܚܘܒܝܢ — *wa-šbōq lan ḥawbayn*, "forgive us our debts," šbq sounding twice in one petition (Peshitta Matt 6:12).[^50^] Biblical Aramaic knows it too: Ezra 6:7, *šĕbûqû* — "let the work of the house of God alone"; Daniel 4:12/20/23 (MT) = Daniel 4:15/23/26 (Eng.) — "leave the stump of its roots in the earth"; Daniel 2:44, hithpeʿel *tištĕbiq* — a kingdom "shall not be left" to another people.[^51^]

Because the word is wide, a certain reading keeps surfacing: *perhaps the cry does not mean abandoned at all.* Syntax settles it — a perfect verb with a first-person object under an interrogative *why* fixes the sense as abandonment; a word does not drag its whole dictionary entry into every sentence. But something far better than assurance is available, from men who stood much closer to the source than we do.

**[lexical evidence]** Greek owns a word that mirrors šĕbaq exactly. ἀφίημι carries both senses — *to dismiss, release, leave, let go*; and *to release from legal or moral obligation, cancel, remit, pardon*. BDAG lists them side by side.[^52^] It is the verb of Matthew 6:12: ἄφες ἡμῖν τὰ ὀφειλήματα ἡμῶν, "forgive us our debts."[^53^] **And Mark uses it, in this same Gospel, for the disciples abandoning him in the garden**: καὶ ἀφέντες αὐτὸν ἔφυγον πάντες — "and they all left him and fled" (Mark 14:50), identical in Nestle, Westcott–Hort, SBL, and Tischendorf.[^54^]

And here is the fact a concordance hands you for free: when the Old Greek translators of the Aramaic scriptures rendered šbq, they reached for ἀφίημι — ἄφετε at 2 Esdras 6:7 (for Ezra 6:7), and again at Old Greek Daniel 4:15 and 4:26.[^55^] Theodotion prefers ἐάω; one occurrence takes ὑπολείπω. **ἐγκαταλείπω is never used for šbq.**[^51^]

| | ἀφίημι (G863) | ἐγκαταλείπω (G1459) |
|---|---|---|
| Core senses | send away, leave, let go, depart | leave behind, desert, leave helpless |
| "Forgive / remit" sense? | Yes — debts, sins (Matt 6:12; 18:32); Thayer: "to let go, give up, a debt" | **No** — absent from Thayer, Abbott-Smith, Vine, Friberg, Mounce, LSJ |
| Mirrors šbq's full range? | Yes — both halves | No — the "leave" half only |
| Mark's usage | 14:50, disciples abandon him; 15:37, "released a great voice" | 15:34, the cry — Mark's only occurrence |
| How Greek translators treat šbq (Ezra/Daniel) | Old Greek's choice (2 Esd 6:7; OG Dan 4:15, 26) | Never |
| How the LXX treats Hebrew ʿāzab | Rare | The chief rendering — "the Sept. for עָזַב" (Thayer) |

### 5.2 He refused it

So when Mark's translator rendered šĕbaqtānî, he had a perfect match sitting in his own vocabulary — a word with the identical double meaning, one he had already used for abandonment fifty-six verses earlier.

**He refused it.**

**[lexical evidence]** At Mark 15:34 and Matthew 27:46 the verb is ἐγκατέλιπες — second singular aorist of ἐγκαταλείπω, in every edition of both gospels.[^56^] It means abandonment and nothing else. Its company in the New Testament: Hebrews 13:5 — "I will never leave you nor forsake you"; 2 Corinthians 4:9 — "persecuted but not forsaken"; 2 Timothy 4:10 and 4:16 — Demas, and then everyone; Romans 9:29, quoting Isaiah 1:9 — the seed "left" to us. And it is the Septuagint's own word at this verse of this psalm: LXX Psalm 21:2 (= 22:1 Eng. / 22:2 MT), ἵνα τί ἐγκατέλιπές με.[^57^]

One thing must be set down openly, because a hostile reader will find it. The standard scholarly explanation is that the gospel verb simply follows LXX Psalm 21:2 — inherited from the Greek Bible, not freshly chosen from the Aramaic.[^47^] If that were the whole story, the "refusal" would be an illusion. But chapter 2 verified the independence directly: the LXX's πρόσχες μοι ("attend to me") has no counterpart in any Hebrew, Targum, or Peshitta witness — and appears in neither gospel. The translation is no lift from the Greek Bible; it is an independent rendering of the Semitic cry, by a translator who demonstrably had ἀφίημι in his pen at 14:50 and again at 15:37. **The same verse proves independence from the LXX and disambiguation of the Aramaic — two decisions, one translator, both verifiable.** The rank must be labeled honestly: the words on the page are direct lexical evidence, checkable in any interlinear; that the refusal was *deliberate* is this study's inference. Targeted searching found no published treatment that poses the ἀφίημι alternative at all — so the observation is offered here, at its own rank, for the council to test.[^30^]

**[morphological observation]** The word he chose is worth opening. ἐγκαταλείπω is λείπω ("leave") compounded twice: καταλείπω, then ἐν-καταλείπω — the lexica form it as ἐν prefixed to the existing compound, and the κατά is usually treated as intensive rather than spatial.[^58^] What the prefixes contribute, per Thayer, is the *in*: "ἐν equivalent to ἐν τινι, in some place or condition" — to leave someone *in* straits, *in* helplessness; Vincent's gloss is the English idiom itself, "left in the lurch." Abandonment down into a situation. Not release from it.

**[lexical evidence]** The Syriac tradition heard what English cannot. Peshitta Mark 14:50 reads ܫܒܩܘܗܝ ܘܥܪܩܘ — *šbaqūhy wa-ʿraqū*, "they forsook him and fled." The disciples do to him, with šbq, the very thing he says God has done. Same root, same Gospel, fifty-six verses apart — invisible in English, invisible in Greek, blazing in Syriac.[^59^] And the Old Syriac Sinaiticus at 15:34 drops the transliteration altogether and simply reads the psalm: ܐܠܗܝ ܐܠܗܝ ܠܡܢܐ ܫܒܩܬܢܝ — the cry and Psalm 22:1 (Eng.) / 22:2 (MT) made one sentence.[^60^] (The Curetonian preserves neither verse; no one may claim it.)[^61^]

### 5.3 The lexical floor, rebuilt

**[correction, stated plainly]** The first edition of this study anchored the floor in a lexical restriction: ʿāzab, it said, describes "the withdrawal of help within a standing relationship," as though the word itself could not carry severance. That claim does not survive its own lexicon. ʿāzab *can* denote outright abandonment — Deuteronomy 31:16–17, Israel "will forsake me and break my covenant," and God answers "I will forsake them and hide my face"; Judges 10:13, "you have forsaken me… I will no longer save you"; 2 Chronicles 15:2; 24:20, "because you have forsaken the LORD, he has forsaken you." BDB even lists *apostatise* among the glosses.[^62^] The restriction was wrong, and this edition withdraws it.

What survives is narrower and stronger, because it rests on things you can inspect rather than on a restriction counterexamples can break.

First, **the severance verbs Hebrew owns and this psalm declines**. *Kārat* — "cut off," the verb of Daniel 9:26 (יִכָּרֵת מָשִׁיחַ, "an anointed one shall be cut off") and of the Torah's karet penalty, the soul "cut off from his people" (Lev 7:20; Num 15:30; Gen 17:14).[^63^] *Gāzar* — Isaiah 53:8, נִגְזַר מֵאֶרֶץ חַיִּים, "cut off from the land of the living."[^64^] Neither root occurs anywhere in Psalm 22. That is not an inference; it is a count. All thirty-two MT verses frame the crisis with abandonment and distance vocabulary — עזב (v. 2), רחק, "be far" (vv. 2, 12, 20) — and never with cutting.[^65^] Daniel 9 and Isaiah 53 had severance vocabulary and used it. This psalm does not.

Second, **ʿāzab's characteristic construction: abandonment *to* a circumstance, not severance *from* a person**. Psalm 16:10 — לֹא־תַעֲזֹב נַפְשִׁי לִשְׁאוֹל, *lōʾ taʿăzōb napšî lišʾôl*, "you will not abandon my soul *to* Sheol"; 2 Chronicles 12:5, "I have abandoned you *into the hand of* Shishak." The preposition points at what you are handed over to.[^66^] Third, the translator's refusal of ἀφίημι, established above. Fourth, the possessive address — אֵלִי, "*my* God," the 1cs suffix as covenant-address form, spoken from inside the bond; Miller and France both read it as relationship asserted at the very moment of felt abandonment.[^67^] Fifth, the question-form itself: the lament *why* is a known instrument of covenant complaint — "Why, O LORD, do you stand afar off?" (Ps 10:1); "why do you hide your face and forget our misery?" (Ps 44:24 Eng. / 44:25 MT) — and Westermann, Brueggemann, and Miller read such protest as spoken *to* God *from within* the covenant, grounded in covenantal loyalty rather than apostasy.[^68^]

**[morphological observation]** Even the *why* is more precise than English shows. לָמָּה is לְ + מָה — *lĕ + mâ*, literally "for what?" The lexica allow both "why" (cause) and "to what end" (purpose), and the לְ is directional.[^69^] Mark renders it εἰς τί; Matthew ἵνα τί, with the LXX — both literally directional, "to what [end]?" The purposive nuance is real and textually grounded; in Koine usage it often flattens to plain "why." Claim it as a floor, not as an exclusive sense.

| # | Support | Where you can check it | Rank |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Severance verbs available and unused — *kārat*, *gāzar* absent from the whole psalm | Dan 9:26; Isa 53:8; full MT of Psalm 22 | lexical evidence |
| 2 | ʿāzab constructs abandonment *to* a circumstance | Ps 16:10 (*napšî lišʾôl*); 2 Chr 12:5 | morphological observation |
| 3 | The bilingual translator's refusal of ἀφίημι | Mark 14:50 vs. 15:34; OG Daniel 4; every lexicon of ἐγκαταλείπω | lexical evidence (facts); inference (deliberateness) |
| 4 | Possessive covenant address — "my God" | אֵלִי, Ps 22:2 (MT); Miller; France | morphological observation + named scholarship |
| 5 | Complaint-form lāmâ — "for what?" | Ps 10:1; 44:24; Westermann, Brueggemann | lexical evidence + named scholarship |

None of these five requires a lexical restriction that a counterexample can break. Together they draw two lines before any theology is consulted. **What the language supports: *abandoned to this, help withheld, left in it alone.* What it does not supply: *severed, no longer in relation.*** That is not a theological conclusion. It is a lexical floor — and everything chapter 10 will build has to stand on it.

## 6. Reading the Psalm He Was Standing In

Chapter 4 established the warrant: quoting the first line of Psalm 22 sets the whole psalm in view — in its conditional form, not as coded triumph. This chapter does the reading: the psalm's shape, a ranked register of its contacts with Golgotha, and one famous verse that must be set down with unusual honesty, because no evangelist ever touched it.

### 6.1 The shape is the argument

**[direct quotation — the arc]** The psalm opens in the dark: אֵלִי אֵלִי לָמָּה עֲזַבְתָּנִי (ʾēlî ʾēlî lāmâ ʿăzabtānî, Psalm 22:1 Eng. / 22:2 MT). It does not stay in simple despair. The complaint has a hinge built into it — וְאַתָּה קָדוֹשׁ (wĕʾattâ qādôš, "yet you are holy," v. 3 Eng. / v. 4 MT) — and the sufferer argues with God from God's own record: "our fathers trusted; they trusted, and you delivered them" (vv. 4–5 Eng. / vv. 5–6 MT). Memory is being used as leverage. Only then comes the humiliation: a worm and not a man, the mockers' taunt, strength poured out like water, a tongue stuck to the jaws, encirclement by dogs, garments divided under his eyes (vv. 6–18 Eng. / vv. 7–19 MT). And at the end of v. 21 Eng. / v. 22 MT the poem breaks mid-verse: עֲנִיתָנִי (ʿănîtānî, "you have answered me"). Kraus noted long ago that the verb negated at the opening ("you do not answer," v. 2 Eng. / v. 3 MT) is affirmed at the turn — the lament closes with its own complaint reversed, and the praise follows from it.[^70^] What that word sounds like against עֲזַבְתָּנִי is the next chapter's work; here it is enough to mark where the psalm turns, because everything the evangelists quote stands on the near side of that turn.

**[ranked register]** The correspondences between psalm and passion are not one kind of evidence. Ranked honestly:

| Psalm 22 (Eng. / MT) | Passion text | What is shared | Rank |
|---|---|---|---|
| vv. 7–8 / vv. 8–9 | Mark 15:29; Matt 27:43 | wagging heads; "let him deliver him… for he desires him" — Matthew tracks LXX Psalm 21:9 nearly word for word | **strong allusion** |
| v. 18 / v. 19 | John 19:24; Mark 15:24 | garments divided, lots cast — John with the fulfillment formula, Mark without | **direct quotation** |
| vv. 14–15 / vv. 15–16 | the crucifixion scene; cf. John 19:28 | thirst, the dried mouth, the failing body | **shared theme** |
| v. 16 / v. 17 | — | quoted by no New Testament writer | **contested text** |

Two rows deserve their evidence shown. Matthew's mockers say πέποιθεν ἐπὶ τὸν θεόν, ῥυσάσθω νῦν εἰ θέλει αὐτόν ("He trusts in God; let him deliver him now, if he desires him"); the Greek psalm reads ἤλπισεν ἐπὶ Κύριον, ῥυσάσθω αὐτόν· σωσάτω αὐτόν, ὅτι θέλει αὐτόν (LXX Ps 21:9 = Ps 22:8 Eng. / 22:9 MT). The verb of trusting differs; the deliverance clause is the psalm's own.[^19^][^71^] And every quotation register of the New Testament's use of this psalm lists vv. 1, 7–8, 18 — and v. 22 in Hebrews 2:12 — and nothing else.[^72^] One caveat belongs to the third row: behind "I thirst" (John 19:28) the standard scholarly candidate is Psalm 69:21, not Psalm 22:15. The echo stays at shared-theme rank, no higher.

**The evangelists work the psalm's first line and its middle. They never touch its sixteenth verse.** That fact is the bridge to the hardest section of this chapter.

### 6.2 The contested verse, set down honestly

**[contested text]** Psalm 22:16 Eng. / 22:17 MT reads, in the Masoretic Text, כָּאֲרִי יָדַי וְרַגְלָי (kāʾărî yāday wĕraglay) — "like a lion my hands and my feet." There is no verb. Every ancient version presupposes one: the LXX has ὤρυξαν (ōryxan, "they dug" — ὀρύσσω means *dig*, not *pierce*, a distinction the philology insists on); the Peshitta reads "they burst through"; Aquila, Symmachus, and Jerome's Hebrew-based Psalter read "they bound"; the Targum keeps the lion but supplies a verb — "biting like a lion."[^73^][^74^] Then there is the scroll. 5/6HevPs, from Naḥal Ḥever, dated to c. 50–68 CE and textually very close to the proto-Masoretic tradition, reads כארו with a final vav — a third-person plural verb. The editors of the official edition, DJD 38, examined the letter under magnification and called the reading "assured."[^75^] A Hebrew manuscript from the evangelists' own century reads a verb here. That must be said plainly.

The counter-considerations must be said plainly too. The same line spells "my hands" ידיה with a final he; Postell and Justiss, in the most recent peer-reviewed survey (2025), argue that a scribe influenced by the two preceding third-person plural verbs would more likely resolve an ambiguous graph *into* a verb — the vav may be sense-making, not archetype — and defend the Masoretic consonants as deliberate poetic polysemy.[^73^] Vall argued the LXX's Vorlage read כארו but traced it back to an original אסרו (ʾāsărû, "they bound") — which would explain why Aquila, Symmachus, and Jerome all translate *bind*.[^76^] Strawn defends the lion, supplying an elided "tear," while conceding that "they bind" has the interpretive edge if one emends at all;[^77^] Swenson defends the Masoretic consonants outright.[^78^] **There is no consensus, and this study pretends to none.** Two theories can be dismissed, though: that the church changed "lion" to "pierced," and that the rabbis hid the piercing. Both are rejected across the scholarly spectrum; the difficulty is very early, very ordinary, and nobody's plot.[^73^]

**[lexical evidence — the silence]** Now the fact that governs the reader of the gospels. Barnes noticed it in 1870: the verse "is nowhere quoted or referred to in the New Testament."[^79^] When John wants a piercing text, he reaches for Zechariah 12:10 — "they will look on him whom they have pierced" (John 19:37) — where the Hebrew verb דָּקָרוּ (dāqārû) genuinely means pierce, as כרה does not.[^80^] The explicit linkage of Psalm 22:16 to the nails begins with Justin Martyr in the mid-second century.[^81^] None of this settles the text-critical question, which stays open. It settles something else: **an argument that needs a contested letter in a verse no evangelist cites was never standing on much.** The psalm's testimony to Golgotha rests where the evangelists built it — vv. 1, 7–8, and 18 — and never on v. 16.

### 6.3 The first family was already praying it

**[strong allusion — an echo, not an anchor]** One more triangle belongs on the table, labeled precisely. Matthew's mockers add a clause the psalm does not have: "for he said, 'I am the Son of God'" (27:43). That clause has a home, and it is Jewish. Wisdom of Solomon 2:18 reads, in verified Greek, εἰ γάρ ἐστιν ὁ δίκαιος υἱὸς θεοῦ, ἀντιλήμψεται αὐτοῦ καὶ ῥύσεται αὐτόν — "if the righteous man is God's son, he will help him and deliver him from the hand of his adversaries."[^82^] The parallel was mapped by Suggs in 1957 and is standard in Matthean scholarship (Nolland).[^83^] The labels matter: Wisdom is an Alexandrian Jewish composition in Greek, deuterocanonical, dated between the first century BCE and the early first century CE.[^84^] What the triangle proves is modest and real: before Golgotha, within Judaism, the mocked righteous man of Psalm 22 was already being read alongside the persecuted righteous son. It proves no literary dependence beyond the verbal overlap, and it anchors nothing that vv. 7–8 do not already anchor. An echo, then — but from the psalm's own first family: when Jesus prayed its first line, he was standing in a prayer his people had long made their own.

That prayer runs from עֲזַבְתָּנִי to עֲנִיתָנִי. How far apart those two words are — and how close they sound — is where we go next.

## 7. The Rhyme at the Turn

The last chapter ended where the psalm turns. This chapter stands at the turn and reports the study's headline finding — rank printed first, because here the rank is half the content. **What follows is a morphological observation, not an inference.** Every premise can be inspected in a pointed Masoretic text; nothing asks you to trust a claim about what the evangelists intended.

### 7.1 The verb the psalm answers itself with

**[direct quotation]** The complaint is specific. Psalm 22:2 Eng. / 22:3 MT: אֱלֹהַי אֶקְרָא יוֹמָם וְלֹא תַעֲנֶה (ʾĕlōhay ʾeqrāʾ yômām wĕlō ṯaʿăneh) — "My God, I call by day, and you do not answer."[^85^] Not *you are absent*. Not *you are angry*. The verb is עָנָה (ʿānâ), "to answer" — Qal yiqtol 2ms, negated.[^86^] This, not forsakenness, is the psalm's own name for its wound.

**[morphological observation]** Now the turn. Psalm 22:21 Eng. / 22:22 MT ends mid-breath: הוֹשִׁיעֵנִי מִפִּי אַרְיֵה וּמִקַּרְנֵי רֵמִים עֲנִיתָנִי (hôšîʿēnî mippî ʾaryēh ûmiqqarnê rēmîm ʿănîtānî) — "Save me from the mouth of the lion; from the horns of the wild oxen — **you have answered me**."[^87^] Same verb. Same root, ענה. Negated at the opening, affirmed at the hinge; the qatal 2ms + 1cs parsing is printed in any interlinear.[^88^] Kraus saw the structure decades ago — "you do not answer" becoming "you have answered me," the lament closed by its own complaint reversed — and it has been standard commentary since (Craigie; Lyons; Paustian, who adds that the verb falls inside one poetic line, so the psalm cannot even be split in two without severing a verse).[^70^][^89^]

English hides most of this, and the difference is a translation choice, not a textual one. At v. 2 Eng. nearly every version reads "you do not answer." At v. 21 Eng. the NASB reads "You answer me," the BSB, WEB, and ASV "answered," the Revised JPS "rescue" with the footnote "Lit. 'answer.'" — while the ESV, NRSV, and HCSB read "you have rescued me" and the NIV reads "save me," either way severing the visible link.[^86^][^90^][^85^] One honesty note: the LXX rendered עֲנִיתָנִי not as "you answered me" but as τὴν ταπείνωσίν μου, "my humiliation," and some scholars prefer emendation.[^90^] This chapter's observation runs on the Masoretic text — the text the cry at Golgotha sounds like, whose consonants no witness here disputes.

### 7.2 The rhyme

**[morphological observation]** There is a second layer, in the sound. Set the three forms side by side:

| Locus | Form | Language / parsing | Root |
|---|---|---|---|
| The cry — Ps 22:1 Eng. / 22:2 MT | עֲזַבְתָּנִי (ʿăzabtānî) | Hebrew, Qal qatal 2ms + 1cs suffix | עזב — "forsake" |
| At Golgotha — Mark 15:34 / Matt 27:46 | שְׁבַקְתַּנִי (šĕbaqtānî) | Aramaic, Peal qatal 2ms + 1cs suffix | שבק — "leave, abandon" |
| The turn — Ps 22:21 Eng. / 22:22 MT | עֲנִיתָנִי (ʿănîtānî) | Hebrew, Qal qatal 2ms + 1cs suffix | ענה — "answer" |

Three words, one grammatical shape: a completed act, second person, with *me* fastened to the end. The shared -tānî is not one morpheme but two — the 2ms subject marker -tā plus the 1cs object suffix -nî — an *identical inflectional build*, not a coincidental syllable.[^88^] And the two Hebrew words open with the same letter, ʿayin, and the same reduced vowel: ʿă-…-tānî → ʿă-…-tānî. **Forsaken-me becomes answered-me, and in Hebrew you can hear it happen. The psalm rhymes its rescue with its wound.**

Two precisions, printed rather than assumed. First, this is paronomasia across two roots, not lexical identity: עזב and ענה share an ʿayin and nothing else; the medial radicals differ. Second, the rhyme is near-perfect, not phoneme-perfect. In the Tiberian reading the tav of עֲזַבְתָּנִי carries dagesh — geminate plosive /tt/, ʿăzab-**tt**ā-nî — while the tav of עֲנִיתָנִי is rafe, fricative /θ/, ʿănî-**ṯ**ā-nî.[^88^] A hearer of the pointed text catches a rhyme with one softened beat. That is what the letters give.

**[rank discipline]** Now the discipline. The root identity at vv. 3 and 22 MT is a fact any interlinear displays; the -tānî correspondence is inspectable in the Westminster Leningrad Codex by anyone who cares to look. What this study does **not** claim is that Mark or Matthew pointed at it; no evangelist comments on the psalm's sound-structure, and saying otherwise would be inventing evidence. What is claimed is narrower and stronger: the psalm was built this way, and the man who quoted its first line was quoting a poem that answers its wound in a word shaped like the wounding word.

The genre warrant, with its caveat. Hebrew poetry plays on sound-pairs pervasively — Casanowicz catalogued several hundred cases in 1893; Noegel and Sasson document pun-based parallelism as a fundamental device of Hebrew verse.[^91^][^92^] But Casanowicz rated the Psalter *low* for wordplay — "in the Psalms only a few are found," the play upon words being "out of place in lyric poetry" — while later scholarship has found notable Psalter cases he missed (Psalm 126's double play on šûb/šĕbût is the standard exhibit).[^91^][^92^] Both are printed; hold both. And one thing said with perfect plainness: more than thirty targeted searches across lexica, commentaries, theses, and journals found **no published source noting the ʿăzabtānî → ʿănîtānî correspondence**.[^93^] It is offered as what it is — a checkable observation standing on its own evidence, not on borrowed authority. If a council member finds it in print, the record will be corrected gladly.

### 7.3 A third layer, flagged unverified

**[open question]** There may be a third layer, flagged rather than asserted. Psalm 22:24 Eng. / 22:25 MT speaks of עֱנוּת עָנִי (ʿĕnût ʿānî) — "the affliction of the afflicted."[^94^] The lexica split the consonants ענה into homonymous roots: BDB's ענה I, "answer," against ענה II/III, "be bowed down, afflicted," from which עָנִי and עֱנוּת derive.[^95^] So the hearer meets ʿ-n-h twice in the "answer" sense (vv. 3, 22 MT) and once in the "affliction" sense (v. 25 MT) — and Delitzsch, on this very verse, stops to disambiguate the two, proof the convergence is audible to careful readers.[^96^] Is it a deliberate pun? No located source calls it one. Hebrew poets exploited exactly such homonym pairs — that much the scholarship supports — but nothing is built on it. It is set down so that you can go and look.

## 8. The Chain the Church Confirmed; The Darkness at Noon

The last three chapters built the whole-psalm reading from the inside. Now the outside question: did anyone in the first century read it that way? One canonical author answers in plain text. One event, usually treated as weather, answers without words.

### 8.1 Hebrews reads the whole psalm as his prayer

**The far side of the turn, in his own voice.**

**[direct quotation]** Hebrews 2:11–12 says the Son is "not ashamed to call them brothers, λέγων (*saying*): Ἀπαγγελῶ τὸ ὄνομά σου τοῖς ἀδελφοῖς μου, ἐν μέσῳ ἐκκλησίας ὑμνήσω σε" — "I will proclaim your name to my brothers; in the midst of the assembly I will sing your praise."[^19^] That is Psalm 22:22 (Eng.) / 22:23 (MT), LXX 21:23 — quoted with a citation formula and placed in the Messiah's own mouth. Not the cry. **The verse after the turn — sung on the far side of "you have answered me."**

The consensus on the speaker is settled: Attridge, Lane, Ellingworth, Koester, and O'Brien all treat the catena of Hebrews 2:12–13 (Ps 22:22; Isa 8:17; 8:18) as the words of Jesus, the text chosen for one word — ἀδελφοῖς, "brothers."[^97^] And because the psalm moves from suffering (vv. 1–21 Eng.) to praise (vv. 22–31 Eng.), that praise sounds on the far side of deliverance: the risen Christ singing in the ἐκκλησία — "my brothers" appears only after the resurrection (John 20:17; Matt 28:10).[^97^]

**[lexical evidence]** One precision: Hebrews opens with ἀπαγγελῶ ("I will proclaim") where the standard LXX reads διηγήσομαι ("I will recount"); the rest of the verse is verbatim LXX.[^19^][^98^]

So the earliest church did exactly what the whole-psalm reading implies. **Verse 1 from the cross. Verse 22 from beyond it.**

**"He was heard" — the link runs through the Greek.**

**[lexical evidence]** Hebrews 5:7 adds its own verdict on the passion prayers: he "offered up prayers and petitions with loud cries and tears to the one who could save him from death — καὶ εἰσακουσθείς, and he was heard." The bridge to Psalm 22 is real and mainstream — Swetnam, Bruce, Easter, and Richardson all settle on Psalm 22 (LXX 21) as background — but it must be drawn on the right line. It runs through the Septuagint of Psalm 21:25 (LXX) = 22:24 (Eng.) / 22:25 (MT): καὶ ἐν τῷ κεκραγέναι με πρὸς αὐτὸν εἰσήκουσέν μου — "when I cried to him, he heard me" — the same verb, εἰσακούω. The Hebrew there is שָׁמֵעַ (šāmēaʿ) — **not** ענה; the LXX rendered עֲנִיתָנִי itself as τὴν ταπείνωσίν μου, "my humiliation." The resonance is real at the level of the psalm's Greek translation; it is not a Hebrew-root link.[^99^]

**[contested text]** And honesty about the answer. "Heard" cannot mean *rescued from dying* — he died. Harnack found the difficulty sharp enough to conjecture a lost οὐκ before εἰσακουσθείς — "he was *not* heard"; no manuscript supports him.[^100^] The dominant modern reading takes ἀπὸ τῆς εὐλαβείας as causal — heard *because of* his reverence — and the answer as salvation *out of* death by resurrection.[^100^][^101^] Which is what the psalm itself says: the answer comes *from the horns of the wild oxen*, not instead of them.

One sentence holds this entire chain: **the first line of the psalm was quoted by Yeshua — the rest of the psalm was quoted by the events.**

### 8.2 The darkness

**Noon to three, and what Luke actually wrote.**

**[direct quotation]** Mark 15:33, Matthew 27:45, and Luke 23:44 agree: darkness over all the land from the sixth hour to the ninth — noon to mid-afternoon. The sun at its highest, and it goes out. Luke alone adds a clause: τοῦ ἡλίου ἐκλιπόντος, "the sun's light failing" — the earliest and best-attested reading (𝔓75, ℵ, B, C*, L); the Byzantine ἐσκοτίσθη ("the sun was darkened"), which the KJV follows, is secondary.[^102^]

**[lexical evidence]** Whatever ἐκλιπόντος means, it cannot describe an eclipse: Passover falls at full moon, when a solar eclipse is astronomically impossible — observed from Origen onward.[^102^][^103^] The evangelists are not reporting weather.

**Amos 8:9–10 — two routes, printed separately.**

**[strong allusion]** Amos 8:9: "'And on that day,' declares the Lord GOD, 'I will make the sun go down at noon and darken the earth in broad daylight.'" Sun down at noon; darkness from noon. Marcus, Evans, Brown, Donahue, and Collins all note the allusion at Mark 15:33.[^104^] Then the next verse — read it slowly — "I will make it like the mourning for an only son." Here the text forks:

| | MT (Hebrew) | LXX (Greek) |
|---|---|---|
| Amos 8:10 | אֵבֶל יָחִיד (ʾēbel yāḥîd) — "mourning for an **only son**" | πένθος ἀγαπητοῦ — "mourning for a **beloved one**" |
| The word's home | יָחִיד is Isaac's word: "your son, your only one" (Gen 22:2, 12, 16); again at Zech 12:10[^105^] | ἀγαπητός is the voice's word: "my beloved Son" (Mark 1:11; 9:7)[^106^] |
| The route to the cross | God mourning his only Son — legitimate, but predominantly homiletic in form (Wiersbe, Akin)[^107^] | The scholarly route: "Only the intertext from Amos declares Jesus still to be beloved of God" (D. A. Smith); so also the Otago thesis[^106^][^43^] |

Do not conflate the two. The academic argument runs through the LXX's "beloved one" to Mark's ἀγαπητός Son; Smith adds that the heavenly voice, heard at the baptism and the transfiguration, is silent here — only the intertext mourns.[^106^] The pulpit argument runs through the MT's יָחִיד to John's μονογενής. Both attested; not the same claim; not the same rank.

**The ninth plague, the ninth hour, the veil.**

**[shared theme]** Behind Amos stands an older darkness: the ninth plague, three days of darkness over Egypt (Exod 10:21–23) — immediately before the death of the firstborn, immediately before Passover. Scholarly literature calls Exodus 10:22 "the closest parallel" to Mark 15:33.[^108^] Darkness, then the firstborn dies, then the Passover. But the full chain — ninth plague, firstborn, beloved Son — is assembled in homiletic writing; each link separately is documented scholarship.[^107^][^108^] Shared theme, not a flagged quotation.

**[lexical evidence]** The hour also speaks. The ninth hour was the hour of the evening tamid and the hour of prayer: Josephus, "about the ninth hour they offered the sacrifice" (War 6.9.3; cf. Ant. 14.4.3); Philo (Spec. Laws 1.169); m. Tamid 3–4; Acts 3:1.[^109^] One caveat: the Mishnah slaughters the lamb mid-afternoon and offers it toward 3:30; "ninth hour" is the conventional approximation.[^109^] At the hour the lamb was offered, the darkness lifts — and he shouts.

**[strong allusion]** Nor does the darkness come alone. The temple veil tears in the same breath (Mark 15:38), and Daniel A. Smith reads the two as coordinated portents — σχίζω, "to tear," binding 15:38 back to 1:10, the heavens torn at the baptism.[^106^] Matthew adds earthquake, split rocks, opened tombs. A sign-complex, not a storm report.

So the ranks, set down openly. Amos at the sixth hour: **strong allusion**. The Exodus–Passover resonance: **shared theme**. Neither is flagged the way the divided garments are flagged. It did not need to be. **Midday darkness is not weather. It is a citation.**

## 9. The Other Two Cries

**[lexical evidence]**

Luke and John do not record the dereliction cry. That is a fact of the texts, and this study will not harmonize it away. But absence is not silence. What each evangelist sets at the moment of death instead — Luke's entrustment, John's completion — is evidence: two discernible editorial responses to one received utterance. Read alongside the Gospel of Peter's rewriting, they show that the cry Mark and Matthew preserved was early, fixed, and uncomfortable.[^16^] A legend does not need to be managed. This utterance was managed.

### 9.1 Luke

**The substitution.**

**[direct quotation / morphological observation]**

Luke 23:46: καὶ φωνήσας φωνῇ μεγάλῃ ὁ Ἰησοῦς εἶπεν· Πάτερ, εἰς χεῖράς σου παρατίθεμαι τὸ πνεῦμά μου ("Father, into your hands I commit my spirit"). This is Psalm 31:5 (Eng.) / 31:6 (MT) — LXX Psalm 30:6 — quoted with three deliberate adjustments, each one inspectable:[^9^][^110^]

| Feature | LXX Psalm 30:6 | Luke 23:46 |
|---|---|---|
| Verb | παραθήσομαι — future, "I will commit" | παρατίθεμαι — present, "I commit" |
| Address | κύριε (in the second colon) | Πάτερ, inserted at the head |
| Second colon | ἐλυτρώσω με κύριε ὁ θεὸς τῆς ἀληθείας ("you have redeemed me, O LORD, God of truth") | omitted |

**[textual evidence]** The tense shift is no copyist's slip; it runs the other way. Byzantine witnesses read παραθήσομαι at Luke 23:46, assimilating Luke back to the psalm's future.[^110^] Scribes moved the text toward the LXX, not away — which is why the present is the harder, earlier reading. Luke's Jesus does not promise a future entrustment. He entrusts, now.

The address is equally deliberate. The psalm says κύριε; Luke's Jesus says Πάτερ — the address of Gethsemane (Luke 22:42). And the omitted colon speaks by its absence: "you have redeemed me, O LORD, God of truth" (אֵל אֱמֶת, ʾēl ʾĕmet — "God of truth," not the paraphrase "faithful God"). The dying man takes the first colon and leaves the redemption clause behind him, unclaimed but not deleted.

**A lens, not a flinch.**

**[theological synthesis]**

The substitution is no flinch. Luke has shaped his entire passion: the centurion declares ὁ ἄνθρωπος οὗτος δίκαιος ἦν — "this man was righteous" (23:47) — where Mark and Matthew hear "son of God"; Luke alone gives the weeping daughters (23:27–31) and the penitent criminal (23:39–43).[^111^] The prayer "Father, forgive them" (23:34a) fits the same pattern, but honesty requires the footnote: omitted by P75, ℵ2a, B, D*, W, and Θ, it stands double-bracketed in NA28.[^112^] We cite it as Luke's atmosphere, not as load-bearing text.

Holly Carey calls Matthew and Luke Mark's earliest documented readers — and reading is itself evidence.[^113^] Foster puts it bluntly: Luke "eradicates the cry of dereliction," substituting "one psalm text (Ps. 22.2) with another (Ps. 31.5)."[^114^] Whether Luke redacted Mark or drew on independent tradition is a live question of composition; it changes nothing about what the swap shows — the cry was there to be responded to.

**A psalm that arrives somewhere.**

**[shared theme]**

Notice which psalm Luke reached for. Psalm 31, like Psalm 22, is a lament that ends in rescue: "Be strong, and let your heart take courage, all you who wait for the LORD" (31:24 Eng.). **Both cries from the cross are the opening moves of poems that arrive somewhere.** Luke did not change the destination. He changed which mile of the road his Jesus speaks from.

### 9.2 John

**One word.**

**[direct quotation / morphological observation]**

John gives one word: Τετέλεσται (19:30) — one word in every Greek edition.[^115^] Perfect indicative middle/passive, third singular of τελέω: not "it is over" but "it has been brought to completion, and stands completed." The perfect tense carries the result forward.

Immediately before it, John records Διψῶ — "I thirst" (19:28) — with his explicit formula ἵνα τελειωθῇ ἡ γραφή, "that the Scripture might be fulfilled."[^115^] Which scripture? The standard candidate is Psalm 69:21 (Eng.) / LXX 68:22: εἰς τὴν δίψαν μου ἐπότισάν με ὄξος, "for my thirst they gave me sour wine to drink."[^116^] Psalm 22:15 (Eng.) / 22:16 (MT) — "my tongue cleaves to my jaws" — is a legitimate secondary echo, but it names no drink. The candidates stand in that order; drafts that reversed the priority stand corrected.

One correction more, recalled from chapter 1: John records no loud voice at all — εἶπεν Τετέλεσται — and, as chapter 1 established, Mark's and Matthew's final loud cry is contentless; only Luke gives the last cry words.[^8^]

**The Psalm 22:31 proposal.**

**[open question]**

Does tetelestai answer the psalm's own last line? Psalm 22:31 (Eng.) / 22:32 (MT) closes: כִּי עָשָׂה (kî ʿāśâ) — "for he has done it," which can genuinely be read "it is done." Some draw the line: Banks, Patterson, Eklund; older devotional writers like F. B. Meyer.[^117^] Set the counterweight down just as plainly. The LXX renders the clause ὅτι ἐποίησεν — ποιέω, not τελέω — so no Greek-verbal link exists; the echo, if it is one, runs through the Hebrew.[^118^] And Brown's exhaustive catalogue of Psalm 22 allusions in the passion omits it entirely.[^117^] A minority proposal — labeled, not asserted, and it stays open.

**Three witnesses, three words.**

**[theological synthesis]**

Mark and Matthew give the desolation. Luke gives the entrustment. John gives the completion. The Gospel of Peter, outside the canon, rewrites the cry itself — "My power, my power, you have forsaken me" (Gos. Pet. 5:19) — a docetic-sounding solution now disputed as a reading but never disputed as a rewriting.[^16^] Two canonical gospels replace or omit; one apocryphon rewords — three independent managements of the same utterance.

**Flatten them into one harmonized quotation and you lose all three.** The evangelists were not stenographers. They were witnesses and readers, each answering the hardest sentence ever recorded — and that three of them reached for an answer is the surest sign the sentence was really there.

## 10. What the Cry Means — and What It Doesn't

Everything before this chapter was excavation. Here the study says what it thinks — in two layers, rank label printed on each, because the layers do not carry the same weight and you deserve to know which is which.

### 10.1 Two evasions resisted

**Not loss of faith; not explained-away anguish.**

**[theological synthesis]** Two opposite evasions should be resisted. The first treats the cry as proof that Yeshua lost faith. But he is praying Scripture, and the *why* is the instrument of covenant complaint — "Why, O LORD, do you stand afar off?" (Ps 10:1) — protest spoken *to* God *from inside* the relationship, not unbelief outside it (chapter 5). And the possessive survives the darkness: אֵלִי, *my* God. France, no foe of the anguish reading, names the register precisely — a relationship "which feels like abandonment."[^119^] Feels — his word, and the right one.

The second evasion is subtler: explaining the suffering so quickly that the cry no longer means what it says. If he was only gesturing at a happy ending, the anguish becomes theatre — and Brown, Hooker, Juel, and Best are right to refuse that domestication. The strongest whole-psalm scholarship refuses it too. Carey's settled position is that reading the cry with the psalm's full context "need not subtract from the sense of Jesus' distress" but *enhances* it.[^113^] Ahearne-Kroll adds: the vindication section is a promise *contingent on the deliverance the lament pleads for* — no rescue can be smuggled in to delete the desolation.[^120^] **The cry must first be allowed to cry.**

### 10.2 The judicial-abandonment reading at its strongest

**The best case, in its own best texts.**

**[testimony / theological synthesis]** There is a reading held with real conviction, from ordinary pulpits to serious theology: at the ninth hour the Son bore, in that cry, the judicial abandonment sin deserves. Give it its best case, because it has one.

Isaiah 53: מֻכֵּה אֱלֹהִים וּמְעֻנֶּה — "stricken, smitten by God, and afflicted" (53:4); וַיהוָה הִפְגִּיעַ בּוֹ אֵת עֲוֺן כֻּלָּנוּ — "the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all" (53:6); תָּשִׂים אָשָׁם נַפְשׁוֹ — "his life a guilt offering," ʾāšām (53:10).[^121^] Galatians 3:13: γενόμενος ὑπὲρ ἡμῶν κατάρα — "Messiah redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us."[^122^] 2 Corinthians 5:21: τὸν μὴ γνόντα ἁμαρτίαν ὑπὲρ ἡμῶν ἁμαρτίαν ἐποίησεν — "he made him who knew no sin to be sin for us."[^123^] The New Testament plainly teaches that he bore what was ours, and this reading hears the cry as the sound of that bearing. **It is not a straw man, and people who love Yeshua hold it from inside those texts.**

Notice how much narrower the honest disagreement is than the loudest versions suggest. **The most careful holders of the judicial reading themselves distinguish abandonment as *experienced* from abandonment as *ultimate*.** Calvin — no one holds the judgment-bearing reading harder — writes in the same section of the *Institutes*: "we do not, however, insinuate that God was ever hostile to him or angry with him. How could he be angry with his beloved Son, with whom his soul was well pleased?" Christ felt, "as it were, forsaken of God," yet "did not cease in the slightest to confide in his goodness" (Inst. II.xvi.11–12).[^124^] Peter Lombard, defending real abandonment at the cross, insists the Godhead "did not dissolve the union."[^124^] The tradition's strongest judicial and union voices meet at one line. He bore what was ours. He was not, in the end, let go.

### 10.3 The two layers, labeled

**Layer one — the lexical floor.**

**[correction, stated plainly / lexical evidence]** A correction in rank, stated plainly: the first edition filed the entire position — floor included — under "theological synthesis." The audit objected, rightly: the floor is not synthesis but inspectable evidence, and this edition promotes it. Chapter 5 rebuilt it on five supports, each one inspectable: the severance verbs Hebrew owns and this psalm never uses (kārat, gāzar — absent from all thirty-two MT verses); ʿāzab's characteristic construction, abandonment *to* a circumstance (Ps 16:10); the translator's refusal of ἀφίημι, the one word that could have softened the cry toward *forgiven*; the possessive covenant address; the complaint-form *why*. None depends on a lexical restriction counterexamples can break — the false claim that ʿāzab *cannot* mean severance was withdrawn in chapter 5 and is not quietly reinstalled here.

What the floor draws are two lines, drawn before any theology is consulted. **What the language supports: *abandoned to this, help withheld, left in it alone.* What it does not supply: *severed, no longer in relation.*** Anyone of any theological position has to build on that floor. Any account of the cross requiring the Father and the Son to have been ontologically separated is not getting that from these words — nor, as 10.2 showed, from Calvin either. What such accounts need, they must import — knowingly, as a theological decision, not smuggled in under cover of an Aramaic verb.

**Layer two — the argued reading.**

**[theological synthesis — the study's own, labeled as such]** Layer two is synthesis, and this study owns it as such. Three witnesses, one direction.

First, the psalm's own commentary on its own cry. Psalm 22:24 (Eng.) / 22:25 (MT): כִּי לֹא־בָזָה וְלֹא שִׁקַּץ עֱנוּת עָנִי וְלֹא־הִסְתִּיר פָּנָיו מִמֶּנּוּ וּבְשַׁוְּעוֹ אֵלָיו שָׁמֵעַ — "he has not despised or abhorred the affliction of the afflicted, and he has not hidden his face from him" — וְלֹא־הִסְתִּיר פָּנָיו מִמֶּנּוּ, wĕlōʾ histîr pānāw mimmennû — "but heard when he cried to him."[^125^] The psalm registered its wound as silence — "you do not answer" (22:3 MT) — and answered it in the same verb at the hinge: "you have answered me" (22:22 MT; chapter 7). If the cry is a citation, the citation comes with its commentary attached — and the commentary says the face was never hidden.

Second, where Paul locates God at the cross. 2 Corinthians 5:19: θεὸς ἦν ἐν Χριστῷ κόσμον καταλλάσσων ἑαυτῷ — "God was in Messiah reconciling the world to himself."[^126^] *In.* At the cross, not turned away from it. And this stands in the same paragraph as 5:21 — Paul carries both clauses at once.

Third, Luke's sequence. The man who cried the dereliction cry next entrusts his spirit into the same hands: "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit" (Luke 23:46; chapter 9). A man does not entrust his spirit to hands he believes have let go of him.

So what is the cry? The sound of a real human being experiencing the felt absence of God while remaining held by him. **Dereliction as experience — not as final fact.** That sentence is a theological synthesis; you have seen what it rests on, and the declined reading at its strongest, in its own best texts. You may weigh it.

**Consequence, not evidence.**

**[testimony — consequence, not evidence]** This is a consequence, not a piece of evidence, and it is labeled so. **The darkness you are standing in is not proof.** The silence is not a verdict. The worst hour any human being ever endured — felt as total absence, through three hours of darkness at noon — was an hour in which God had not moved an inch. He has not hidden his face. He heard.

He did not only endure the dark. He mapped it, marked the exit, and handed us the map with his last strength. That is why he shouted.

## 11. The Psalm's First Family; The Ascent; Notes on Method

The study closes in three movements: where this psalm lived before Golgotha and where it has lived since; what the reading obligates; and how every claim in these pages has been labeled, so that nothing here was smuggled past you.

### 11.1 First family

**[testimony — reception history]** Psalm 22 was Israel's before it was the church's, and it remains both. Long before the evangelists set its first line at Golgotha, generations of sufferers had already prayed it — because Israel's prayer book makes room for the worshipper who begins at *why* and refuses to pretend otherwise. Three readings carry that history, and each can be checked at its source.

David's own distress. The superscription itself claims him: מִזְמוֹר לְדָוִד (mizmôr lĕdāwid, "a psalm of David," MT v. 1). Ibn Ezra reads the cry as Davidic autobiography — אֵלִי אֵלִי "uttered at times when he was in distress."[^127^] Radak records the same option: David fleeing Saul.[^128^]

Persecuted Israel. Rashi hears the nation: "They are destined to go into exile, and David recited this prayer for the future."[^129^] Radak makes the national reading programmatic — the singular address gathers "the people of Israel as a whole… as one man in exile and of one mind" — and it is exiled Israel that calls out אֵלִי אֵלִי לָמָּה עֲזַבְתָּנִי (ʾēlî ʾēlî lāmâ ʿăzabtānî, Psalm 22:1 Eng. / 22:2 MT).[^128^]

Esther. In the Bavli, Esther crosses the inner court toward Ahasuerus, the Divine Presence withdraws from her, and "she immediately said: 'My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?'" — then prays on through the psalm, past the dog to the lion's mouth (b. Megillah 15b, quoting Psalm 22:1 Eng. / 22:2 MT and 22:20–21 Eng. / 22:21–22 MT).[^130^] The name on the psalm's door was read as hers: at b. Yoma 29a Esther is the hind and the dawn — אַיֶּלֶת הַשַּׁחַר (ʾayyelet haš-šaḥar), the word of the superscription — and Midrash Tehillim states it flatly: "'Ayelet ha-shaḥar' — this is Esther."[^131^][^132^] The psalm is prayed on Purim to this day: Sephardi and Oriental communities open the evening service with it, and the Vilna Gaon's rite keeps it as Purim's Psalm of the Day.[^133^]

**[open question — it changes the section's use, not its truth]** Here the precision this council edition owes. Berkovitz has shown that the Esther linkage crystallized in late antiquity, partly as a counter-narrative to Christian claims on the psalm — Esther as countertype of Christ, the exegetical contest traceable from the late second century.[^134^] So the Esther tradition cannot be cited as pre-Christian background for what Mark did; it postdates him, and this edition does not ask it to do work it cannot do. What it proves instead is exactly what this section exists to prove: **long after Golgotha, in full view of the church's claim, Israel kept praying this psalm as its own** — in the night before risking everything, at the feast of near-annihilation survived. Reception, not background. The section is stronger for saying so.

None of this is a rival to be defeated. A psalm given to every righteous sufferer is exactly the kind of psalm the Righteous Sufferer would pray. The church's reading does not evict the psalm's first family; it joins the house that psalm built. **Any use of this chain that curdles into contempt for the people whose prayer book it is has misread the chain from the first link.**

### 11.2 The ascent

**[theological synthesis — fruit, not evidence]** Aliyah means going up — up to Jerusalem, up to the Temple, up to the platform to read the Word. It is always upward, and always toward the presence. But this psalm does not begin its climb from a comfortable place. It begins at *I am a worm and not a man* (22:6 Eng. / 22:7 MT), in the dust of death — and it does not get out by denying where it is. It goes all the way through, and the going-through is what produces the view.

So here is what this study obligates. **You are permitted to say verse 1.** Not as a failure of faith. Not as something to repent of afterward. The Son of God, at the summit of his obedience, prayed *why have you forsaken me* out loud, in public, at full volume — and Scripture recorded it without embarrassment and without correction. Whatever you were taught about which prayers you are allowed to bring: that got settled at Golgotha. The complaint is in the canon. It has a tune.

And the second thing, which only makes sense because of the first. **Verse 1 is not the last verse.** The psalm never goes back to soften the complaint and never calls it an overreaction; it simply arrives somewhere else — and it arrives by answering the exact word it started with. *You do not answer* becomes עֲנִיתָנִי (ʿănîtānî, "you have answered me," 22:21 Eng. / 22:22 MT). He has not hidden his face. He heard. You may name the distance without surrendering the address.

And when you cannot yet reach the praise at the end, you do not have to manufacture it. The congregation can hold verse 22 for you until you can sing it yourself. That is what a congregation is *for* — and verse 22, *in the midst of the congregation I will praise you*, is the one verse of this psalm the New Testament puts in the risen Messiah's mouth: ἀπαγγελῶ τὸ ὄνομά σου τοῖς ἀδελφοῖς μου, ἐν μέσῳ ἐκκλησίας ὑμνήσω σε (Hebrews 2:12).[^135^] **He is not waiting for you at the end of the psalm. He is standing in the room, singing the part you cannot reach yet.**

### 11.3 Notes on method

**[method]** A word about the claim-labels used throughout — you will meet them in every chain.

| Label | What it hands you | Example in this chain |
|---|---|---|
| **direct quotation** | the text explicitly cites, usually with a formula; highest confidence | Ps 22:1 in Mark 15:34; Ps 22:18 in John 19:24; Ps 22:22 in Heb 2:12 |
| **lexical evidence / morphological observation** | an observation about the words themselves, inspectable with an interlinear, requiring no inference | ἀφίημι available and declined at Mark 15:34; ענה negated at 22:2 Eng. / 22:3 MT, affirmed at 22:21 Eng. / 22:22 MT |
| **strong allusion** | no citation formula, but verbal and situational overlap close enough that the author is clearly working with the text in view; high confidence, still an inference | Ps 22:7–8 in Matt 27:39–43; Amos 8:9 behind the noon darkness |
| **shared theme** | the texts illuminate each other and belong to the same world of imagery, without demonstrable literary dependence | the thirst of Ps 22:15 alongside John 19:28 |
| **theological synthesis** | an argued conclusion drawn from multiple texts, which no single verse states outright; labeled as mine, the support shown, and yours to weigh | the cry read as felt dereliction rather than ontological abandonment |
| **testimony** | an experiential claim offered as witness, not as evidence; it asks to be recognized, not verified | the first family and the ascent above |
| **contested text** | the manuscripts genuinely diverge and honest scholars disagree | Ps 22:16 — handled openly, deliberately kept out of the load-bearing argument |
| **open question** | the evidence genuinely does not decide, and this study declines to pretend otherwise | Matthew's ʾēlî or Mark's ʾĕlāhî; how the Elijah confusion works in Mark |

Three housekeeping labels sit outside the table, because they mark the edition accounting for itself rather than the evidence: *correction* (chs. 5, 10, 12), *method* (this section), *structural* (ch. 12). And where the fact at issue is a manuscript reading rather than a lexeme or an echo, the chapters print *textual evidence* (chs. 1, 9, 12).

**The rule this book runs on: you should always be able to tell which of these you are being handed. If you ever can't, that is a defect in the writing, not a subtlety in the argument.**

One convention to state outright. Wherever Hebrew text is displayed, both numbering systems are given — Psalm 22:1 (Eng.) / 22:2 (MT) — because the Hebrew Bible counts the superscription as verse 1 and English translations do not. A study that shows you pointed Hebrew under English numbering has quietly stopped being careful. In running text the parentheses are sometimes dropped for rhythm — 22:2 Eng. / 22:3 MT instead of Psalm 22:2 (Eng.) / 22:3 (MT); this is an accepted stylistic variant, the order is always English first and MT second, and no verse is ever left in a single numbering system where the Hebrew text itself is in view.

## 12. Corrections Register and Verification Appendix

This last chapter is the accountability record, and it is written to be read first. Two correction passes stand behind this edition: the Opus audit of the shipped study — four confirmed errors, plus its headline finding: the ʿăzabtānî → ʿănîtānî rhyme the shipped study missed, now chapter 7 — and a fresh research pass over the second-edition draft, which found five more. Every item below carries its disposition — confirmed, fixed, reopened, or restricted to testimony — and every disposition can be checked against the sources named.[^136^] Nothing here is settled by assertion; that is the point of the chapter.

### 12.1 Against the shipped study

**[confirmed error ×4, all fixed]** The audit's frame: every draft in the repository worked from Matthew, and that frame let four checkable errors survive review. Their dispositions:

| # | Error (shipped) | Verdict | Disposition in this edition |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Matt 27:46 labeled "(Aramaic, in Greek letters)" | Confirmed error | Fixed — label now "mixed"; vernacular argument moved to Mark (§12.1.1) |
| 2 | Elijah confusion presented as settled | Confirmed error | Fixed — reopened as Mark-vs-Matthew crux; open question (§12.1.2) |
| 3 | Pointed Hebrew under English verse numbers | Confirmed error | Fixed — dual versification wherever Hebrew is displayed (§12.1.3) |
| 4 | LXX sentence implied the citation follows the LXX | Confirmed error | Fixed — replaced with the πρόσχες μοι divergence (§12.1.4) |

**The "(Aramaic)" label on Matthew 27:46.**

**[morphological observation]** The shipped file printed Ἠλὶ ἠλί under an Aramaic label. Ἠλί is Hebrew — ʾēlî (אֵלִי); Aramaic is ʾĕlāhî, which Mark transcribes ἐλωΐ. Matthew's cry is **mixed**: Hebrew address, Aramaic verb (šĕbaqtānî). Not pedantry: the authenticity argument beneath it was refuted by its own verse — in Matthew the address *is* the scriptural Hebrew. Fixed as prescribed: label "mixed"; the vernacular argument runs on Mark's fully Aramaic ἐλωΐ ἐλωΐ; Matthew's Hebraized address kept as a real variation.[^137^]

**The Elijah confusion.**

**[open question]** The shipped study treated the Eli/Eliyahu sound-play as obvious. It is, in Matthew. In Mark the address is ἐλωΐ, the resemblance to Ἠλίας markedly weaker — yet Mark reports the identical confusion. Reopened as a crux: Matthew preserves the older form and Mark Aramaized; or Matthew Hebraized to sharpen the wordplay; or the confusion was never really phonetic. Each has defenders; Buth's study is the specialist treatment.[^137^] It stays open — and honestly handled it became evidence: Mark kept the harder form anyway (ch. 3).

**Versification.**

**[textual evidence]** The shipped study displayed pointed Masoretic Hebrew under English verse numbers — "Psalm 22:1 (Hebrew)" over what in the MT is 22:2, with the same offset at the hinge (MT 22:22). English numbering is a defensible house convention; labeling pointed Hebrew with it is not. Fixed: **Psalm 22:1 (Eng.) / 22:2 (MT)** wherever Hebrew is displayed.[^65^]

**The Septuagint sentence pointed the wrong way.**

**[lexical evidence → textual evidence]** The shipped sentence — ἐγκατέλιπες "is the Septuagint's own word for this exact verse" — was true as a verb claim, false in its implication that the citation follows the LXX. LXX Psalm 21:2 reads ὁ θεὸς ὁ θεός μου, **πρόσχες μοι**· ἵνα τί ἐγκατέλιπές με, "attend to me" — words standing in no Hebrew manuscript and in neither gospel.[^20^] The citation is no lift from the Greek Bible: independent, Semitic-based. That stronger fact now occupies the paragraph (ch. 2), anchored in Karrer.[^21^]

### 12.2 Against the second-edition draft

The audit corrected the shipped study; the research pass corrected the corrector's draft. Five findings, stated plainly.

**"All four record a loud voice" — false for John.**

**[textual evidence]** John 19:30 has no loud-voice note: εἶπεν Τετέλεσται. The loud voice belongs to the Synoptics — Mark 15:34, 37; Matthew 27:46, 50; Luke 23:46.[^8^] Corrected (chs. 1 and 9), and the correction sharpened the finding: the final loud cry in Mark and Matthew is contentless; only Luke gives it words.

**The ʿāzab lexical floor.**

**[lexical evidence]** The draft's framing — ʿāzab (עָזַב) denotes withdrawal within a standing relationship — fails as a lexical restriction: Deuteronomy 31:16–17, Judges 10:13, 2 Chronicles 15:2; 24:20 use the verb for outright abandonment of covenant partners.[^62^] The rebuilt floor stands on five supports: severance verbs (kārat, gāzar) available but nowhere in Psalm 22; ʿāzab's characteristic construction of abandonment *to* a circumstance (Ps 16:10); the translator's refusal of ἀφίημι, the one Greek word mirroring šbq's full range; the possessive "my God"; and the complaint-form lāmâ, the lament-*why* of covenant protest (Ps 10:1; 44:24) (ch. 5).

**Reference repairs.**

**[textual evidence]** Biblical Aramaic šbq: Ezra 6:7 (not 6:6) and Daniel 4:12/20/23 MT (= Eng. 4:15/23/26), plus Daniel 2:44.[^49^] Matthew's lemma is ηλει in the NA tradition (Byzantine ηλι); citing NA28, this edition writes ηλει ηλει.[^138^]

**The "remez" label.**

**[open question]** The term belongs to the medieval PaRDeS scheme; no rabbinic text names such a rule. The practice — identifying psalms by their opening words — is genuinely ancient (m. Tamid 7:4); the label is later.[^139^] This edition drops "remez" as a term of art and grounds the whole-psalm reading where it can be checked: the psalm's own ענה hinge and the evangelists' demonstrated use of the whole psalm (chs. 4 and 7).

**Citations and priorities.**

Carey's monograph is *Jesus' Cry from the Cross*, LNTS 398 (London: T&T Clark, 2009) — not Mohr Siebeck.[^113^] "I thirst" (John 19:28) belongs to Psalm 69:21; Psalm 22:15 is a secondary echo; the priority is now printed in that order.[^116^] The Esther loci are split: recitation at b. Megillah 15b; the ayyelet ha-shaḥar identification at Yoma 29a with Midrash Tehillim 22.[^130^]

### 12.4 Structural notes for the module

**ChainAnchor needs a witness field.**

**[structural]** `ChainAnchor` is (book, chapter, verse) with no witness field. Error 3 is that gap shipped as content; error 4 is unrepresentable without it — there is nowhere to put LXX Psalm 21:2 as a distinct witness with its own numbering and its own πρόσχες μοι. The audit's proposal stands: `val witness: String = "MT"` on `ChainAnchor`, defaulted. It breaks nothing today and lets Chain №1 eventually say *this citation matches the Hebrew against the Greek* — for Psalms citations, frequently the entire point.[^136^]

**ChainMember.evidence: from discipline to enum.**

**[structural]** `ChainMember.evidence` is free-text on an open vocabulary. Four labels have held across twenty-five members — real discipline, but nothing enforces it. Make it an enum and the four-tier promise becomes structural rather than dependent on vigilance.[^136^] This edition applies the same discipline to itself: every claim in this chapter carries its rank, and the register closes with no item undischarged.
