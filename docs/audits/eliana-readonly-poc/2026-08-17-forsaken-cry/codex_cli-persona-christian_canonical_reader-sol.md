# Genuine reaction

What struck me most was the module’s insistence that the cry belongs simultaneously to real anguish and deliberate mission:

> “Purpose does not erase suffering. Suffering does not erase purpose.”

That is the sentence I would remember and repeat. It refuses two distortions at once. Yeshua is not acting out pain for an audience, but neither has the Passion suddenly become an event whose meaning is hidden from him. The cross is where the mission is endured, not where the mission is forgotten.

The strongest exegetical observation is equally memorable:

> “The psalm is already on the hill before Yeshua names it.”

The divided garments, shaking heads, and trust-taunt make Psalm 22 more than a verse Yeshua happens to remember during pain. The scene has already been narrated inside the psalm’s world. When Yeshua then cries its opening line, he identifies what is happening. That does not require the speculative claim that every bystander mentally recited every verse. It requires only the ordinary recognition that an opening line can deliberately invoke the work to which it belongs—especially when several unmistakable features of that work are already present.

The wilderness comparison deepened this for me. Matthew first gives us:

> “If you are the Son of God…”

as a demand that Yeshua prove sonship through spectacular action. At Golgotha the challenge returns: prove who you are by coming down. The module’s conclusion is right:

> “Because he is the Son, he does not save himself from the work by which he saves others.”

That is canonical synthesis, as the module admits, but it is not an imaginative ornament laid over the text. Matthew has supplied the beams: the repeated challenge, the demand for self-rescue, Scripture used as pressure, and Yeshua’s refusal to establish sonship on the adversary’s terms. The connection reveals something important about obedience: rescue from the trial would not necessarily vindicate the Son; here it would mean abandoning the work the Son came to accomplish.

## Where the module was especially responsible

The study’s distinctions among several meanings of “forsaken” are genuinely useful. It is right to separate:

- nonrescue from the cross,
- genuine human desolation,
- sin-bearing and judgment,
- filial repudiation,
- and metaphysical rupture within God.

Those are not interchangeable claims. The cry plainly establishes suffering and nonrescue. Wider canonical argument is needed to speak about atonement, judgment, and sin-bearing. Nothing in the grammar of *azavtani*, *sabachthani*, or *enkatelipes* proves that the Trinity fractured.

The module also rightly resists turning the end of Psalm 22 into a device for canceling its beginning. Its formulation is exact:

> “Do not use the ending to silence the beginning.  
> Do not use the beginning to erase the ending.”

Yeshua’s appeal to the whole scriptural world of Psalm 22 does not make “Why have you forsaken me?” less real. Psalm 22 itself refuses that reduction. Its movement toward hearing and worldwide praise does not reveal that the opening anguish was false; it shows that anguish was not the psalm’s final judgment about God.

The discussion of Psalm 22:16 is another instance of earned caution. The module acknowledges the real textual problem—“like a lion” and the ancient forms behind “pierced” or “dug”—and declines to build its argument on a disputed reading. That strengthens the study. The Psalm 22 connections in Matthew do not depend on forcing that variant.

Likewise, the cautions concerning *remez* are warranted. Calling the cry a remez-like invocation may describe the literary effect well. Claiming that Yeshua was demonstrably applying a formally codified PaRDeS rule would exceed the historical evidence. The study loses nothing by distinguishing those claims.

The warning against a simplistic “God cannot look at sin” account is also necessary. Habakkuk 1:13 does not teach that God is metaphysically unable to behold evil, and Psalm 22:24 explicitly says that God did not hide his face from the afflicted one. Whatever an orthodox atonement account means by judgment or forsakenness, it cannot casually contradict the psalm Yeshua invoked.

At the same time, the module avoids making one atonement model synonymous with Christianity. It permits argument about sacrifice, ransom, curse-bearing, judgment, and reconciliation while drawing the classical Christian boundary at an actual fracture in the divine being. That distinction is doctrinally serious without pretending that all orthodox Christians describe the mechanism of atonement identically.

## Where the caution began to perform itself

The module occasionally acts as though affirming Yeshua’s deliberate use of Psalm 22 would require illicit access to his private psychology. That is most visible here:

> “He chooses — or, at minimum, Matthew deliberately gives him — the opening of a specific psalm.”

The retreat to “or, at minimum, Matthew deliberately gives him” is too severe for the book’s own confessed frame. Matthew is not presenting a fictional speech assigned to a character whose historical agency must remain inaccessible. He reports Yeshua speaking the line. Elsewhere in the same Gospel, Yeshua repeatedly interprets his mission, predicts his death and resurrection, cites context-bearing Scripture, embraces the cup in Gethsemane, and continues the wilderness pattern of faithful sonship. Across the canonical Gospels, his cross-speech is again scriptural and purposeful. John even explicitly joins physical need, conscious purpose, and scriptural fulfillment:

> “Knowing that everything had now been accomplished, so that the Scripture might be fulfilled, [he] said, ‘I thirst.’”

That canonical characterization is evidence of agency. It does not give us an exhaustive transcript of Yeshua’s consciousness, but an exhaustive transcript is not needed to recognize intentional speech. The alternative implied by the hedge—perhaps Yeshua only emitted anguish while Matthew supplied the interpretive purpose—belongs more naturally to a historical-critical reconstruction that brackets the Gospel’s own testimony than to this module’s stated Christian reading.

A similar excess appears in:

> “It still does not give us a transcript of Yeshua’s interior reasoning.”

True, but repeatedly invoking that boundary creates the impression that intentionality can be affirmed only after gaining private psychological access. Ordinary reading does not work that way. We infer purpose from speech, action, context, repeated characterization, and narrative pattern. Here those forms of evidence converge unusually strongly.

The study itself eventually says more confidently:

> “The cry is not a closed answer.  
> It is a deliberate interpretive address:  
> **Read what is happening here through this prayer.**”

I believe that conclusion is better calibrated than some of the earlier grading. It identifies the cry as intentional without claiming that Yeshua silently supplied an exegetical commentary on every verse or that every historical hearer understood the signal fully.

The module should therefore distinguish two claims more sharply:

1. **Yeshua deliberately invokes Psalm 22 as the scriptural world through which his death should be understood.**  
   The Gospel and canonical pattern license considerable confidence here.

2. **Yeshua intended every hearer to retrieve and consciously apply every movement of the entire psalm.**  
   That stronger claim remains an inference and should be stated more carefully.

The present draft sometimes hedges the first claim as though only the second were available.

## A few phrases I would refine

“The mockers quote the psalm before Yeshua does” is rhetorically powerful, but “quote” may be slightly too exact. Matthew 27:43 closely echoes or revoices Psalm 22:8; it is not introduced as a conscious citation, and the wording is adapted to the scene. The theological irony remains intact if the module says:

> “The mockers speak the psalm’s taunt before Yeshua gives its opening line.”

That is just as memorable and more precise.

I would also treat this sentence carefully:

> “The cross is not a fracture in God’s life. It is the terrible, unified act by which God enters and overcomes the alienation of sin.”

The first sentence articulates a necessary trinitarian boundary. The second is compelling Christian synthesis, but “the” unified account should not be allowed to flatten orthodox differences about how judgment, satisfaction, sacrifice, victory, and reconciliation relate. The module mostly avoids that problem by labeling the statement as synthesis; that label should remain prominent.

Finally, the draft repeats its evidence grades enough times that the method begins to compete with the passage. “Steel,” “strong inference,” and “theological synthesis” are helpful once. Repeated too frequently, they make the reader watch the author certify each sentence instead of allowing the cumulative scriptural case to bear its own weight. Some of that apparatus belongs in the council notes rather than the devotional body.

## What I would carry away

I would repeat these four things:

- Psalm 22 was already being enacted around Yeshua before he spoke its first line.
- The cry is genuine lament, not a coded triumphalism that makes the agony unreal.
- Because the speaker is the Messiah who knowingly came to this hour, the lament is also a purposeful act of scriptural self-location.
- Psalm 22 itself teaches us neither to deny the abyss nor to make the abyss God’s final word: the afflicted one cries that God is far, keeps saying “my God,” and finally declares that God heard.

The pastoral ending also deserves to remain:

> “Let Scripture form you before the darkness, so that when pressure strips away performance, what remains is language deep enough to hold both the wound and the destination.”

That grows naturally from Yeshua’s life rather than turning his suffering into a slogan. The accompanying warning—not to tell every sufferer that every wound was specifically assigned as necessary cargo—is wise and earned.

## Final judgment: earned caution and performed caution

The **specific hedge** is:

> “He chooses — or, at minimum, Matthew deliberately gives him — the opening of a specific psalm,”

together with the repeated insistence that we lack a “transcript of Yeshua’s interior reasoning” and therefore must grade whole-psalm invocation only as a guarded inference.

The **canonical pattern** licensing greater confidence is not private access to Yeshua’s mind. It is his consistently purposeful, context-bearing use of Scripture; his repeated predictions of his death and resurrection; his stated interpretation of his life and blood; his willing acceptance of the cup; Matthew’s repeated Son-of-God testing pattern; the accumulation of Psalm 22 correspondences before the cry; the other scriptural sayings from the cross; John’s explicit union of suffering and fulfillment; and Hebrews’ placement of Psalm 22:22 in the Son’s mouth.

That pattern licenses the confident conclusion that **Yeshua deliberately located his suffering and mission within Psalm 22 and invoked more than an isolated sentence of pain**. It does not prove that every hearer retrieved every verse or that every detail of the psalm was consciously encoded as a one-to-one prediction.

The caution was **earned** where the module refused to derive trinitarian rupture from grammar, preserved the reality of Yeshua’s suffering, acknowledged the Psalm 22:16 variant, qualified the historical use of *remez*, distinguished canonical synthesis from direct quotation, and resisted making one atonement formulation the only Christian reading.

The caution was **performed** where it repeatedly treated Yeshua’s deliberate invocation of Psalm 22 as though affirming it required inaccessible psychological knowledge. The Gospel’s sustained characterization and the canon’s coherent pattern are already evidence. Within the Christian frame this module explicitly confesses, the text could—and should—say with less embarrassment what its own conclusion finally says: the forsaken cry was genuine anguish, and it was a deliberate interpretive act.
