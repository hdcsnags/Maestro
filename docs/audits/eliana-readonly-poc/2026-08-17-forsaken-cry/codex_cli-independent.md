# Independent editorial and theological review

## 1. Overall assessment

This is a serious, textually attentive, and pastorally responsible study with a persuasive central argument: Matthew intends Jesus’ cry to be heard within the larger language-world of Psalm 22, and neither the reality of his suffering nor the continuing integrity of his mission should be erased.

The module is strongest when it distinguishes what the text explicitly says from literary inference and doctrinal synthesis. Its handling of Psalm 22, the warning against deriving Trinitarian metaphysics from verbal tense, and the refusal to turn lament into theatrical anguish are especially sound.

Its principal weakness is cumulative overargument. The essay repeatedly restates the same conclusion through slightly different frameworks—“steel,” “scriptural self-location,” “go home with the quotation,” wilderness testing, canonical comparison, five meanings of forsakenness, remez, and the refusal to come down. Individually these are useful; together they make the study feel like several overlapping studies joined without enough compression.

There is also a recurring tension between the module’s commendable epistemic caution and several claims stated more confidently than the evidence allows. Most notably:

- That the historical mockers unknowingly “quote” Psalm 22.
- That Jesus “takes” the weaponized psalm and intentionally turns it toward God.
- That Psalm 22:24 directly rules out every relevant sense of the Father hiding his face.
- That the cross can straightforwardly be described as judging sin while the relevant atonement argument is only gestured toward.
- That Hebrews’ use of Psalm 22:22 materially strengthens a claim about Jesus’ conscious intention in Matthew 27.

The module should be retained, but shortened substantially and revised so that its own distinctions between narrative presentation, historical reconstruction, canonical theology, and interior intention are observed consistently.

## 2. Cross-cutting findings

### The central distinction is excellent

The module’s most valuable contribution is its separation of claims commonly compressed into the word *forsaken*:

> “Yeshua suffered”; “Yeshua was not rescued”; “Yeshua experienced desolation”; “The Father actually ceased to be present to or united with the Son.”

That distinction is exegetically and pastorally important. It prevents a narrative fact—Jesus was not delivered from crucifixion—from automatically becoming a psychological diagnosis or a theory of intra-Trinitarian rupture.

The later six-part reading tool makes the same distinction usable rather than merely conceptual. This is the module’s clearest original teaching contribution.

### The argument is more cumulative than cohesive

The study promises to be a single essay-length module, but it often reads like multiple treatments of the same question:

1. Psalm 22 is already active in Matthew 27.
2. The wilderness temptation returns at the cross.
3. The entire psalm may be invoked.
4. Scriptural formation explains simultaneous spontaneity and intention.
5. The other Gospels show purposeful scriptural speech.
6. “Forsaken” conceals multiple theological claims.
7. The “Father turned away” doctrine is challenged.
8. Remez is reconsidered.
9. Refusal to descend interprets sonship.
10. The reading method is generalized.

The result is not incoherence, but diminished force through repetition. The main case is effectively established by the end of “The psalm was already in the air.” Much of what follows could be reduced by a third to a half.

### Epistemic labels are useful but become a rhetorical badge system

The metal-grading language—“steel,” “strong inference,” “theological synthesis”—is initially helpful. It teaches readers to distinguish direct textual evidence from interpretation.

Repeated too often, however, it starts to feel like an internal certification system:

> “This connection is steel.”

> “This is steel within the Christian canon.”

> “Keep the metal labeled.”

> “The best argument does not need a false badge.”

This language risks substituting declared confidence levels for demonstration. “Steel” is also rhetorically stronger than the actual category “direct quotation”: a quotation may be secure while its function remains disputed.

Recommendation: introduce the grading tool once, use ordinary terms throughout the essay, and reserve the formal hierarchy for “The shovel” or a concise sidebar.

### The study sometimes crosses boundaries it has carefully drawn

The module correctly says:

> “What can be said about what the evangelist is doing? What can be said about Yeshua’s interior consciousness? Those are not always the same claim.”

Yet it frequently moves between those levels:

> “They do not know they are doing it.”

> “Yeshua takes the text the mockers have weaponized and turns it vertically toward God.”

> “He completes the mission they think the lack of rescue has disproved.”

The first statement claims access to the mockers’ awareness. The second claims Jesus’ intentional rhetorical response. The third again reconstructs the mockers’ interpretation. These are plausible narrative readings, but not established historical psychology.

The language should consistently identify the level of the claim:

- “Matthew depicts the mockers as unwittingly speaking the psalm’s language.”
- “Within Matthew’s literary arrangement, Jesus’ cry turns their taunt into prayer.”
- “The narrative invites readers to see the absence of rescue as fulfillment of mission rather than disproof of sonship.”

### “The mockers quote the psalm” needs qualification

Matthew 27:43 closely echoes Psalm 22:8, but “the mockers quote the psalm” is slightly too strong if it suggests a conscious or formally marked citation. There is no citation formula, and the words are integrated into the taunt. “Echo,” “reuse,” or “speak in the language of” is safer.

There is also a source-critical complication the essay does not need to solve but should respect: the historical mockers’ exact words and Matthew’s scripturally shaped narration are not identical questions. The module acknowledges this distinction elsewhere but states “the mockers quote first” as a historical event more than as a feature of Matthew’s presentation.

### Psalm 22:24 is made to settle more than it can settle

The study repeatedly treats:

> “He did not hide His face from him; when he cried to Him, He heard”

as the psalm’s decisive correction of the opening cry. It certainly establishes that abandonment is not the psalm’s final verdict. But it does not necessarily mean that the speaker’s earlier experience of divine distance was unreal, or that the psalm excludes a period of unanswered lament before deliverance.

The phrase can be retrospective: God ultimately heard and did not finally reject the sufferer. It does not by itself resolve whether there was temporary nonintervention, experienced absence, judgment, or concealment within the lament’s dramatic movement.

Accordingly, Psalm 22:24 strongly challenges a claim of final repudiation. It does not single-handedly disprove every theological use of “hidden face” language at the cross.

### The treatment of atonement is underdeveloped relative to its confidence

The essay says:

> “The cross truly judges sin.”

> “The Son truly bears what he came to bear.”

> “It is the terrible, unified act by which God enters and overcomes the alienation of sin.”

These are recognizable Christian theological claims, but the precise content remains unclear. Does “judges sin” mean penal substitution, representative judgment, Christus Victor, sacrificial expiation, covenant judgment, or something else? “Bears what he came to bear” is deliberately broad but rhetorically sounds conclusive.

The study is right not to become a full atonement treatise. It should therefore either:

- use more modest language, such as “the wider New Testament associates his death with sin-bearing, sacrifice, judgment, reconciliation, and victory,” or
- briefly acknowledge that Christian traditions construe the relation among those categories differently.

At present, the essay criticizes one popular penal formulation while relying on adjacent judgment language without defining its own position.

### Classical Trinitarian boundaries are basically sound but need sharper formulation

The rejection of a fracture in the divine being is well founded within classical Nicene Christianity. However:

> “The Father ceased to be Father toward the eternal Son”

is not a normal claim even among most proponents of penal substitution, so it risks refuting an extreme formulation rather than the strongest opposing position.

The more difficult question is not whether the Trinity “became two,” but what theologians mean by judicial abandonment, loss of consolation, or the incarnate Son’s experience of Godforsakenness while the divine communion remains undivided.

The module recognizes these categories but does not explore them enough to establish its preferred boundary. It should distinguish:

- ontological rupture, which classical Trinitarian theology excludes;
- relational opposition within the economy of judgment, which some traditions affirm in qualified language;
- withdrawal of sensible consolation or experienced presence;
- non-deliverance from suffering and death.

That would make the argument more exact and less likely to sound like a broad dismissal of substitutionary traditions.

### Canonical comparison is valuable but occasionally overextended

Luke 23:46, John 19:28, John 16:32, Hebrews 2:12, and Hebrews 9:14 can enrich a Christian canonical reading. The module usually labels this move appropriately.

Still, those texts cannot simply be used as transparent explanations of Matthew’s intent:

- John 16:32 says the Father is with Jesus, but applying it directly to Matthew’s cry requires canonical theological harmonization.
- Hebrews 2:12 places Psalm 22:22 in the Son’s mouth, but that does not prove that Matthew’s Jesus consciously invoked the entire psalm.
- Hebrews 9:14 describes Christ’s self-offering through the eternal Spirit, but the precise force of “eternal Spirit” is debated and should not bear too much Trinitarian weight without comment.
- Second Corinthians 5:19 is better rendered along the lines of “God was reconciling the world to himself in Christ.” The common wording “God was in Christ” can misleadingly sound like a direct statement about divine presence inside Jesus rather than the manner or sphere of reconciliation.

The canonical material supports theological coherence; it does not settle historical intention.

### The study handles the hard question best when it leaves it open

The section “Did Yeshua think the Father had abandoned him?” is one of the strongest because it resists two reductions:

> “Yeshua mistakenly believed the Father was gone.”

and:

> “Yeshua was merely signaling victory and did not mean the question.”

The resulting formulation—

> “Yeshua voices genuine dereliction by placing the moment inside a prayer whose destination is hearing, praise, and the reign of God”

—is a productive theological reading, provided “placing” is understood at least at the narrative level. It preserves the question rather than explaining it away.

The module should trust that openness more. It sometimes undercuts it by returning to more settled declarations about unified divine purpose.

### The pastoral instincts are strong

The module is especially responsible in these cautions:

> “Purpose does not erase suffering.”

> “Do not use the ending to silence the beginning.”

> “It does not give us permission to explain every person’s trauma from above.”

These protect sufferers from two common forms of spiritual harm: treating anguish as insufficient faith and assigning a providential explanation to another person’s trauma.

The final application—formation in Scripture before crisis—is constructive and does not reduce lament to technique.

The pastoral limitation is that the essay’s sustained polemical and methodological analysis may exhaust readers before they reach that landing. A grieving reader may encounter more argument about allowable propositions than companionship in the cry. For a closed study group this may be acceptable, but the facilitator should know that this is primarily an exegetical-theological essay, not a devotional meditation.

### Repeated rhetorical moves become templated

Several devices recur beyond their useful limit:

- “Purpose does not erase suffering; suffering does not erase purpose.”
- “Do not use X to erase Y.”
- “The text gives us enough.”
- “Go home with the quotation.”
- “Keep the metal labeled.”
- “The cry is not X; it is Y.”
- “The psalm is already on the hill.”
- “One English word has been carrying too many suitcases.”

The antithetical sentences are memorable, but their frequency makes the prose sound engineered. The essay should choose two or three controlling refrains and retire the rest.

The mixed imagery also accumulates: locked doors, keys, the psalm “in the air,” steel and metal grades, suitcases, a shovel, visible beams, a longer fuse. Each image works locally, but together they reduce tonal cohesion.

### Working renderings require careful final review

The draft wisely flags this. Particular phrases needing attention include:

- “Far from my rescue are the words of my roaring.”
- “They curl the lip.”
- “He rolled himself onto YHWH.”
- “You lay me in the dust of death.”
- “All are from One” in Hebrews 2:11.

Some are defensible literal renderings but sound alien or may imply disputed syntactical decisions. “He rolled himself onto YHWH” is especially awkward and may distract from the argument. Any later claim built on “delight,” “hide his face,” “heard,” or “from One” must be checked against the final translation.

## 3. Section-by-section notes

### “The sentence that became an answer before it became a question”

**Assessment: Strong opening, but slightly overpolarized.**

The three opening readings efficiently establish the interpretive field. The distinction among suffering, nonrescue, experienced desolation, repudiation, and metaphysical rupture is excellent.

The formulations are somewhat caricatured:

> “This is not really anguish at all; it is a coded announcement that the psalm ends in victory.”

Few careful whole-psalm readings would deny anguish altogether. Likewise, “the human Jesus finally collapsing” loads the psychological reading with an implication of failure. State the alternatives in forms their advocates would recognize.

“The first locked door” and “the second” introduce a metaphor that is not used again. Cut it or make it structurally meaningful.

### “The tool: scriptural self-location”

**Assessment: Conceptually useful; terminology needs restraint.**

“Scriptural self-location” is a strong teaching term because it describes the proposed phenomenon without claiming an ancient technical category.

The discussion of metalepsis is appropriate. The handling of *remez* is cautious, but the term may create more historical burden than benefit. The statement:

> “A Jewish word often used for something similar is remez”

needs scholarly support or more hedging. Contemporary Christian teaching often uses *remez* more broadly and more technically than historical evidence permits. Since “metalepsis” and “scriptural self-location” already do the work, *remez* could be confined to a brief note.

### “The words Matthew preserved”

**Assessment: Strong philological restraint.**

This section properly distinguishes Hebrew *azavtani*, Aramaic *sabachthani*, and Greek transliteration. The statement that the exact spoken form is beyond recovery is appropriately cautious.

The best sentence is:

> “Grammar will not do the theology for us.”

The discussion of qatal and aorist is useful, though it may be more technical than the eventual theological payoff warrants.

“Both identify its meaning with Psalm 22:1” is essentially right as literary identification, but technically the evangelists translate the saying rather than provide a formal source citation. “The wording identifies the cry with the opening of Psalm 22” would be more exact.

### “Suffering is not the same proposition as abandonment”

**Assessment: Strong theological framing.**

The distinction between purpose and painless experience is carefully made:

> “Knowledge is not anesthesia. Consent is not immunity.”

This is memorable without being glib.

The sentence:

> “The choice has already been placed on the record before the nails”

should not imply that Gethsemane exhausts the complexity of willing obedience or that consent makes every later aspect psychologically transparent.

The “fully human / not merely human” formulation is orthodox in intention, but “not merely human” is less precise than explicitly naming the module’s Christological commitment: fully divine as well as fully human.

### “The psalm was already in the air”

**Assessment: The exegetical center and strongest section.**

The cumulative correspondences—garments, head-shaking, trust-taunt, opening cry—make a persuasive case that Matthew narrates the crucifixion in Psalm 22’s language.

Problems of overstatement include:

> “The mockers quote the psalm before Yeshua does.”

Better: “Matthew gives the mockers words closely echoing Psalm 22 before Jesus speaks its opening line.”

> “They do not know they are doing it.”

This is a plausible effect of Matthew’s irony, not recoverable historical knowledge.

> “The psalm is giving Matthew language for the enemies.”

This is a sound literary conclusion, though it should be recognized as a claim about Matthew’s composition, not necessarily a transcript of independent speech.

The reference to “a recent literary argument about Mark” should either identify the scholar in the body or be removed. An unnamed scholarly proposal adds authority without giving readers enough to evaluate it.

### “The first temptation returns”

**Assessment: Persuasive literary synthesis, slightly too expansive.**

The repeated “If you are the Son of God,” the demand for demonstrative rescue, and scriptural pressure support the proposed parallel. The module correctly labels it synthesis rather than direct evidence.

The claim:

> “The first temptation has returned in its final form”

is rhetorically stronger than the evidence. “Matthew’s earlier sonship test appears to return in climactic form” preserves the force without implying a demonstrable compositional intention.

“Force the Father’s hand” aptly connects the temple temptation to testing God, but the cross mockers are demanding that Jesus save himself or be rescued; the parallel is not exact. The section admits this, which is good.

This treatment could be shortened. The Deuteronomy backgrounds have already been explained in “The tool.”

### “Read the psalm he named”

**Assessment: Excellent reading of the psalm’s unresolved movement.**

The section does not flatten Psalm 22 into either despair or victory. Particularly strong is:

> “The memory of divine faithfulness can comfort a sufferer. It can also sharpen the pain.”

The treatment of Psalm 22:16 is admirably restrained and may prevent a common apologetic overclaim. It is somewhat tangential, but worth keeping as a concise note if the audience is likely to know the “pierced” controversy.

The main required revision concerns Psalm 22:24. Saying it is “the psalm’s own interpretation of what happened” is reasonable, but the subsequent use should acknowledge that this is the psalm’s retrospective or final testimony. It does not necessarily erase a preceding period experienced as unanswered abandonment.

### “Did Yeshua mean the whole psalm?”

**Assessment: One of the strongest sections.**

The five live possibilities are fair, and the evidence grades are appropriately differentiated.

The use of Hebrews 2:12 supports a Christian canonical reading of Psalm 22 beyond verse 1. It does not materially prove Jesus’ conscious intention in Matthew 27. The module mostly respects that boundary, but:

> “Hebrews hears the speaker who suffered in Psalm 22 as the Son”

is a little broader than the immediate evidence. Hebrews directly places verse 22 in the Son’s mouth; whether it thereby identifies every part of the psalm’s original speaker in an uncomplicated way is a further hermeneutical conclusion.

“Yeshua told everyone to recite the entire psalm” is usefully downgraded, though the module later returns to language close to this claim.

### “Chosen or spontaneous is the wrong fight”

**Assessment: Pastorally and psychologically perceptive.**

The rejection of a simple intention-versus-reflex binary is compelling:

> “A person formed by Scripture can speak reflexively and intentionally at the same time.”

“Formation is intentionality with a longer fuse” is memorable, though philosophically it may overstate matters. Formation can shape spontaneous speech without every resulting utterance being intentionally communicative in the same sense.

The claim that Scripture is part of how Jesus “understands and performs his mission” is plausible as Gospel characterization. “Performs” may sound theatrical in a section guarding against theatrical suffering; “enacts” would be better.

### “The other dying words”

**Assessment: Useful canonical guardrail.**

The warning against mashing the Gospels into “one featureless transcript” is excellent. The precision note about Matthew 27:50 is important.

Luke and John show that scriptural speech and genuine suffering are compatible in Gospel theology. They do not independently prove that Matthew 27:46 is intended as a whole-psalm cue. The section generally observes this.

John 19:28’s syntax and punctuation have interpretive complexities: “so that Scripture might be fulfilled” may relate to Jesus’ knowledge and action, but the exact relationship deserves modest wording.

### “Five different claims hiding inside ‘forsaken’”

**Assessment: The most useful systematic section; needs refinement in claims 3–5.**

#### Claim 1: The Father did not rescue Jesus from the cross

**Strong.** This is a clear narrative fact, provided “rescue” means removal from crucifixion before death rather than vindication through resurrection.

The resurrection should be mentioned because Christian readers may otherwise hear “none comes” as absolute rather than “no rescue from undergoing death comes.”

#### Claim 2: Jesus entered genuine desolation

**Strong with appropriate limits.** The warning against turning the saying into a complete clinical report is excellent.

“Experienced desolation” remains an inference from the utterance, not a narrated psychological description. The section acknowledges that and should maintain “plausibly” or “at least at the level voiced by the lament.”

#### Claim 3: Jesus bore judgment and sin within his mission

**Theologically plausible but underargued.** “The cup and the darkness carry judgment associations” needs references and hedging. Darkness has judgment associations, but its precise function in Matthew 27 is debated.

“Substantial canonical support” is fair, but the traditions’ different constructions of sin-bearing should be named briefly.

#### Claim 4: The Father repudiated or ceased to love the Son

**The negative conclusion is sound, but the supporting argument is too quick.** Psalm 22:24 and John 16:32 weigh against final rejection. They do not alone adjudicate every qualified account of judicial opposition or experienced dereliction.

#### Claim 5: The Father and Son were metaphysically separated

**Strong within classical Trinitarian commitments.** The text does not say this, and ontological fracture is incompatible with classical divine unity.

The phrase “the Trinity temporarily became two” is vivid but targets a crude position. Address the stronger claim: whether an economic or judicial forsakenness can be affirmed without ontological division.

### “Did Yeshua think the Father had abandoned him?”

**Assessment: Strong, appropriately unresolved.**

This section models the restraint the rest of the module advocates. It neither dismisses the cry nor treats it as transparent metaphysical reportage.

The preferred conclusion is strong:

> “Yeshua voices genuine dereliction by placing the moment inside a prayer whose destination is hearing, praise, and the reign of God.”

For maximum precision, “Matthew places the cry inside…” may be the strongest historical-literary claim; “Yeshua places…” requires a conclusion about Jesus’ intention.

### “The doctrine people often import”

**Assessment: Necessary corrective, but it risks becoming denominationally adversarial.**

The reading of Habakkuk 1:13 is correct and important: the verse does not teach that God is literally incapable of seeing evil.

The treatment of the “turned face” slogan should distinguish popular hymn or sermon language from carefully formulated penal-substitution accounts. Not every use of the metaphor implies ontological rupture or cessation of paternal love.

> “The cross truly judges sin”

is asserted rather than developed.

> “God was in Christ reconciling the world to Himself”

should be replaced with a translation less vulnerable to misunderstanding: “God was reconciling the world to himself in Christ.”

The appeal to Hebrews 9:14 is suggestive, but “through the eternal Spirit” should not be used as an uncomplicated proof of explicit Trinitarian cooperation without noting interpretive debate.

### “Remez: ‘Recite the whole thing’”

**Assessment: Honest but redundant.**

This largely repeats “Did Yeshua mean the whole psalm?” and the earlier grading scheme. The caution is good:

> “The synthesis can be right without becoming steel.”

But the essay has already made this point. Merge the best paragraph into the earlier section and remove the duplication.

The phrase “recite the whole thing” is stronger and more specific than the evidence. “Hear the larger psalm” or “retrieve the psalmic context” is more defensible than literal recitation.

### “The answer hidden in the refusal to come down”

**Assessment: Theologically compelling, with one logical overstatement.**

The Matthean irony in “He saved others; he cannot save himself” is well handled. The core claim—that self-rescue would conflict with completion of his mission—is narratively well supported.

> “The inability is not lack of power. It is the moral necessity of love, obedience, and mission.”

This is theological interpretation, as the module admits. “Moral necessity” could raise unnecessary questions about divine necessity and freedom. “The ‘cannot’ is narrated as fidelity to mission, not simple lack of power” would be safer.

“Because he is the Son, he does not save himself” is effective but should not imply that sonship logically requires this exact atonement mechanism without the broader Gospel argument.

### “Where the text aims”

**Assessment: Strong pastoral landing.**

The outsider/believer distinction is respectful and clear. “At minimum, this is more than random verbal debris” is true but rhetorically dismissive of no serious alternative; “a deliberately scriptural presentation” would be better.

The application to scriptural formation is excellent. The warning against assigning every trauma a divinely necessary purpose is among the module’s most important pastoral safeguards.

> “His life is … a tutorial in how truth is inhabited under pressure”

risks turning the unique Passion too quickly into moral example. The paragraph does preserve the unique rescue claim, but the transition could be more careful: participation and imitation follow from, rather than compete with, the unique salvific claim.

### “The shovel: how to read a cry that quotes Scripture”

**Assessment: Excellent as a reusable study tool, but it partly repeats the essay.**

The eight steps are clear and transferable. Steps two, six, seven, and eight are especially valuable.

“Go home with the quotation” may not always mean reading an entire biblical book or assuming every part of the source is activated. Add a caution: context recovery should identify relevant source features without presuming that quotation imports every detail.

“Does this speaker habitually use isolated slogans?” sets up too simple an opposition. Ancient scriptural citation can be locally apt without invoking a broad context, and judgments about contextual activation should be made case by case.

The “audit salience” step is excellent editorial guidance but arrives late. It could help shape the module itself: the present study makes abandonment the controlling frame for a long time even while warning against doing so.

### “The chain, standing back”

**Assessment: Useful summary with one overconfident conclusion.**

The chain clearly distinguishes the principal textual connections.

The final statement:

> “It is a deliberate interpretive address: ‘Read what is happening here through this prayer.’”

is stronger than the preceding evidence grade. At the level of Matthew’s literary presentation, it is persuasive. At the level of Jesus’ historically conscious communicative intent, it remains inference.

Revise to:

> “In Matthew’s presentation, the cry functions as an interpretive address: read what is happening through this prayer.”

### “A prayer”

**Assessment: Theologically coherent and pastorally strong, but slightly argumentative.**

The prayer successfully carries the study’s central balance into devotion. Particularly effective are:

> “Give me the honesty to say ‘why,’ the relationship to keep saying ‘my God’…”

and:

> “Keep me from turning anguish into theater.”

Some lines continue debating the study’s opponents rather than praying:

> “Keep me from turning anguish into a fracture in You.”

This may be appropriate for the module, but a prayer that allows more silence and less recap would feel less like the argument restated in devotional syntax.

> “By the Spirit who carried him through the offering”

draws on Hebrews 9:14 but makes a specific interpretation of “eternal Spirit.” If retained, it should align with the theological position explicitly established in the essay.

### “Research notes for council review”

**Assessment: Valuable internal apparatus; should not appear in the published module.**

The publication posture is clear and appropriately self-aware. The claims that should survive review are mostly sound.

Required revisions:

- Claim 3 should say the mockers’ words “strongly echo” rather than unqualifiedly “invoke” the psalm.
- Claim 6 should say Hebrews places Psalm 22:22 in the Son’s mouth, rather than implying that it settles the identity of the entire psalm’s speaker.
- Claim 7 should retain the retrospective or final character of Psalm 22:24.
- “The whole psalm is intentionally invoked” may be a strong inference at the level of evangelistic literary function but only a plausible inference about Jesus’ conscious historical intent.
- “Felt abandonment” should remain plausible, while noting that the cry itself constitutes abandonment-language whether or not modern affective categories map neatly onto it.
- The scholarly checks should be completed before publication, especially the Jewish reception review and verification of every working rendering.

The council questions are well chosen. Question 1 identifies the largest editorial issue: the study argues against making abandonment controlling while devoting most of its length to precisely that frame.

## Recommended revision priorities

1. Cut approximately 25–35 percent, primarily by merging:

   - “Did Yeshua mean the whole psalm?” with “Remez.”
   - “The first temptation returns” with “The answer hidden in the refusal to come down.”
   - “The other dying words” with the canonical evidence in the five claims.
   - “The shovel” with a shorter concluding method box.

2. Consistently distinguish:

   - Matthew’s literary presentation.
   - The historical words and intentions of Jesus.
   - The historical awareness of the mockers.
   - Later canonical Christian interpretation.
   - Classical doctrinal synthesis.

3. Replace “the mockers quote Psalm 22” with “Matthew gives the mockers language closely echoing Psalm 22,” except where the stronger wording is explicitly defended.

4. Present Psalm 22:24 as the psalm’s final or retrospective testimony, not as proof that no meaningful abandonment or concealment could have occurred within its movement.

5. Define the module’s atonement claims or state them more modestly. Do not critique “turned face” language while leaving “judges sin” and “bears what he came to bear” undefined.

6. Reduce the repeated metal, shovel, suitcase, locked-door, air, beam, and fuse metaphors. Retain one controlling method metaphor and one central refrain.

7. Verify every working translation and every quotation-dependent argument before closed testing, especially Psalm 22:1, 8, 24; Hebrews 2:11–12; John 19:28; and 2 Corinthians 5:19.

With those changes, the module would retain its genuine strengths: exegetical care, doctrinal seriousness, epistemic restraint, and unusually responsible pastoral treatment of suffering.
