# The Forsaken Cry

### Aliyah: The Ascent — Study 1  
*Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani? — The Psalm Already on the Hill*

> **Draft note for integration:** Scripture renderings in this draft are working renderings made for the study. Replace them with the confirmed Eliana text before publication, then re-run every quotation-dependent claim against the final wording.

---

## The sentence that became an answer before it became a question

Few sentences in Scripture have been made to carry more doctrine than this one.

Some hear it and say: *This is the moment the Father turned away from the Son.*

Some hear it and say: *This is the human Jesus finally collapsing into the feeling that God had abandoned him.*

Some hear it and say: *This is not really anguish at all; it is a coded announcement that the psalm ends in victory.*

All three readings begin by trying to answer the sentence.

I want to begin one step earlier.

Why these words?

Yeshua has Scripture enough for every kind of pain. Psalm 31 gives him a prayer of trust. Psalm 69 gives him the voice of the persecuted. Psalm 88 ends in darkness. Job gives him page after page of protest. Yet Matthew and Mark preserve this cry:

> **Ἠλί Ἠλί λεμὰ σαβαχθανί;**
>
> *Ēli, Ēli, lema sabachthani?*
>
> **“My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”**  
> — Matthew 27:46

And the sentence is not floating alone over Golgotha.

Before Yeshua says the opening line of Psalm 22, the people around the cross have already been acting out the psalm. They divide his clothes. They shake their heads. They mock his trust in God with words drawn from the psalm itself.

The psalm is already on the hill before Yeshua names it.

That is the first locked door.

The second is the word *forsaken*. We have been letting one English word carry several different claims as though they were identical:

- Yeshua suffered.
- Yeshua was not rescued from the cross.
- Yeshua experienced desolation.
- Yeshua believed the Father had repudiated him.
- The Father actually ceased to be present to or united with the Son.
- The life of God was somehow torn in two.

Those are not one proposition.

The first two are written across the Passion in blood. The others require argument.

This study is an attempt to slow the sentence down before doctrine, psychology, or devotion runs away with it.

The question is not whether Yeshua suffered. He did.

The question is what his suffering permits us to conclude about the Father, the Son, and the purpose being carried through the suffering.

And the key is the same key Aliyah keeps handing you:

> **When Scripture is quoted, go home with the quotation.**

---

## The tool: scriptural self-location

A quotation can do more than decorate speech.

It can identify the kind of moment a speaker believes he is standing inside.

In the wilderness, Yeshua answers three temptations with three passages from Deuteronomy. Those passages are not random religious slogans. Their home contexts concern Israel in the wilderness: bread, testing, worship, and the question of whether the son will trust YHWH where the first son failed.

Yeshua does not merely find verses that sound relevant. He locates his testing inside Israel’s story.

That is what I mean in this study by **scriptural self-location**:

> **A speaker uses an older text not only to describe a feeling, but to locate the present event inside the older text’s world.**

A modern literary term for the effect is **metalepsis**: one quoted line pulls a larger source-text into the new scene.

A Jewish word often used for something similar is **remez** — a hint.

I am going to use that word, but with a warning.

The familiar four-level *PaRDeS* scheme — *peshat, remez, derash, sod* — is a later systematization. We should not claim that Matthew 27 documents Yeshua applying a formally named first-century rule called *remez*. That would be history by enthusiasm.

But if by *remez* we mean this:

> **a deliberate hint that sends the hearer back to a larger passage**

then the word names the question well.

Did Yeshua merely express anguish in borrowed language?

Or did he give the hearer the first line so the hearer would retrieve the psalm?

The answer will not come from the label. It will come from the text.

---

## The words Matthew preserved

Here is Matthew’s sentence in full:

> **Περὶ δὲ τὴν ἐνάτην ὥραν ἀνεβόησεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς φωνῇ μεγάλῃ λέγων· Ἠλί Ἠλί λεμὰ σαβαχθανί; τοῦτ’ ἔστιν· Θεέ μου θεέ μου, ἱνατί με ἐγκατέλιπες;**
>
> *Peri de tēn enatēn hōran aneboēsen ho Iēsous phōnē megalē legōn: “Ēli, Ēli, lema sabachthani?” tout’ estin: “Thee mou, thee mou, hinati me enkatelipes?”*
>
> “About the ninth hour Yeshua cried out with a loud voice, saying, ‘Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?’ — that is, ‘My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?’”  
> — Matthew 27:46, working rendering

Matthew does something unusual: he preserves the sound of the Semitic cry and then translates it for the Greek reader.

The source line in the Hebrew Psalter is:

> **אֵלִי אֵלִי לָמָה עֲזַבְתָּנִי רָחוֹק מִישׁוּעָתִי דִּבְרֵי שַׁאֲגָתִי׃**
>
> *Eli, Eli, lamah azavtani; rachoq mishu‘ati, divrei sha’agati.*
>
> “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Far from my rescue are the words of my roaring.”  
> — Psalm 22:1 in English numbering; Psalm 22:2 in the Hebrew numbering, working rendering

Notice the difference.

The Hebrew psalm has **azavtani**, from the Hebrew root עזב, “leave” or “forsake.”

The Gospel cry has **sabachthani**, representing an Aramaic form from the root שׁבק, which can carry the same sense.

Matthew’s **Eli** looks closer to the Hebrew form; Mark preserves **Eloi**, a more plainly Aramaic-looking form. The evangelists give us Greek transliterations of a Semitic cry, not an audio recording, and the exact spoken form is beyond recovery.

That uncertainty costs us nothing essential.

Both evangelists translate the cry for us. Both identify its meaning with Psalm 22:1.

This connection is **steel: direct quotation**.

But grammar will not do the theology for us.

The Hebrew **azavtani** is a qatal form. Matthew’s Greek translation **enkatelipes** is an aorist. Neither fact proves that the abandonment was final, metaphysical, relational, temporary, perceived, judicial, or complete in every possible sense.

The forms present the forsaking as real within the speaker’s cry.

They do not tell us what happened inside the Trinity.

And the repeated address — **“my God, my God”** — should not be rushed past either. The complaint is spoken *to* the one being accused. The line holds abandonment-language and continuing address side by side.

What that continuing address means is interpretation.

The text itself gives us the tension.

---

## Suffering is not the same proposition as abandonment

Let me state this as plainly as I can:

> **Yeshua’s suffering does not prove that the Father repudiated him.**

The Passion leaves no room to minimize the suffering. Yeshua is betrayed, abandoned by disciples, falsely accused, mocked, struck, scourged, exposed, crucified, and left without the rescue his enemies demand.

Purpose does not erase any of that.

But neither does suffering erase purpose.

Matthew has spent the journey to Jerusalem telling the reader that the cross is not an accident that overtakes Yeshua before he can complete his work:

- He repeatedly predicts his death and resurrection.
- He says the Son of Man came to give his life as a ransom for many.
- At the meal he interprets the cup as his covenant blood poured out for many for forgiveness.
- In Gethsemane he asks whether the cup can pass and then consents: **“not as I will, but as you.”**

The choice has already been placed on the record before the nails.

That does not make the pain less real. Knowledge is not anesthesia. Consent is not immunity. A mission can be willingly entered and still contain an abyss no advance description can make painless.

But the mission means we cannot treat the cry as though the Gospel has suddenly forgotten who the speaker is, why he came, or what he has repeatedly said will happen.

Christian confession makes the tension sharper, not easier.

Yeshua is fully human, so the agony cannot be theater.

Yeshua is not merely human, so his identity and mission cannot be erased in order to make the scene psychologically relatable.

The task is not to choose humanity or Christology.

The task is to refuse to diminish either one.

## The psalm was already in the air

Now return to Matthew 27 and begin several verses before the cry.

### The garments

> “After crucifying him, they divided his garments by casting lots.”  
> — Matthew 27:35

Psalm 22 says:

> “They divide my garments among themselves, and for my clothing they cast lots.”  
> — Psalm 22:18

Matthew does not stop to announce the connection. He expects the scene to carry it.

### The heads

> “Those passing by blasphemed him, shaking their heads.”  
> — Matthew 27:39

Psalm 22 says:

> “All who see me mock me; they curl the lip; they shake the head.”  
> — Psalm 22:7

### The trust-taunt

Then the chief priests, scribes, and elders say:

> “He trusted in God; let God rescue him now, if he delights in him — for he said, ‘I am God’s Son.’”  
> — Matthew 27:43

Psalm 22 says:

> “He rolled himself onto YHWH — let Him deliver him! Let Him rescue him, since He delights in him!”  
> — Psalm 22:8, working rendering

This is the strongest point.

The mockers quote the psalm before Yeshua does.

They do not know they are doing it. That is Matthew’s irony. They weaponize the psalm’s language against the man hanging before them, unaware that their own mouths are placing him inside its scene.

Then, three verses later, Yeshua gives the psalm’s opening line.

He is not introducing Psalm 22 into a scripturally empty moment.

He is naming the poem the hill has already begun to recite.

The sequence is:

- Psalm 22’s garments.
- Psalm 22’s head-shaking.
- Psalm 22’s trust-taunt.
- Psalm 22’s opening cry.

A recent literary argument about Mark goes further and proposes that Mark arranges the Psalm 22 allusions in reverse order, moving from garments and mockery backward until Yeshua reaches the psalm’s first line. Whether or not every detail of that proposal persuades you, the larger fact is not controversial:

> **The Passion scene is being narrated in the language-world of Psalm 22.**

That is stronger than saying, “A suffering man remembered a familiar lament.”

It still does not give us a transcript of Yeshua’s interior reasoning.

It tells us how Matthew and Mark ask their readers to hear the event.

That is the level of evidence we actually possess.

## The first temptation returns

Matthew has shown us this test before.

Immediately after the baptismal voice names Yeshua as the beloved Son, the tempter approaches him with:

> **“If you are the Son of God…”**  
> — Matthew 4:3, 6

The demand is proof through self-serving power.

Turn stones into bread.

Throw yourself down and force angelic rescue.

Accept the kingdoms by worshiping the wrong ruler.

The devil even quotes a psalm — Psalm 91 — to make presumption sound like faith.

Yeshua answers with Deuteronomy.

But look at the home contexts:

- Deuteronomy 8:3 concerns Israel’s hunger, manna, and learning that life depends on YHWH’s word.
- Deuteronomy 6:16 recalls Massah, where Israel tested YHWH by demanding proof of His presence.
- Deuteronomy 6:13 commands exclusive worship in the same wilderness instruction.

The Son answers the wilderness by entering Israel’s Scripture and remaining faithful where Israel failed.

Now listen to Golgotha:

> **“If you are the Son of God, come down from the cross.”**  
> — Matthew 27:40

The first temptation has returned in its final form.

Prove sonship by rescue.

Force the Father’s hand.

Make identity visible by escaping suffering.

And again Scripture is being wielded at him — this time Psalm 22’s trust-taunt.

The scenes are not identical. In the wilderness Yeshua directly rebuts an adversary. At the cross he prays to God. One is counter-speech; the other is lament.

But the literary pattern is difficult to miss:

- “If you are the Son of God.”
- Scripture used as pressure.
- A demand for spectacular rescue.
- Yeshua refusing to prove sonship on the adversary’s terms.
- Yeshua speaking from inside Scripture.

In the wilderness he answers the tempter horizontally.

At the cross he takes the text the mockers have weaponized and turns it vertically toward God.

He does not come down.

He does not perform for them.

He completes the mission they think the lack of rescue has disproved.

This is not direct quotation evidence. Matthew never inserts a narrator’s note saying, “The cross repeats the wilderness test.”

Call it what it is:

> **Strong literary-theological synthesis.**

But it is synthesis with several visible beams holding it up.

## Read the psalm he named

Now go home with the quotation.

Psalm 22 is not a neat movement from sadness to a motivational ending. It is a long prayer in which experience, memory, accusation, petition, and praise struggle in the same body.

### The abyss

The psalm opens without softening:

> “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?  
> Why are you far from my rescue, from the words of my roaring?  
> My God, I call by day, and you do not answer;  
> by night, and there is no rest for me.”  
> — Psalm 22:1–2, working rendering

The speaker does not say merely, “I am sad.”

He says God is far.

He says he cries and receives no answer.

The language of forsakenness belongs to the psalm. No responsible Christian reading should bleach it out because the ending becomes brighter.

### The remembered God

But the psalm does not stop addressing God.

> “Yet you are holy, enthroned on Israel’s praises.  
> In you our fathers trusted; they trusted, and you delivered them.”  
> — Psalm 22:3–4

This is not a simple statement of serene confidence. It intensifies the complaint.

*You delivered them. Why not me?*

The memory of divine faithfulness can comfort a sufferer. It can also sharpen the pain when the remembered pattern seems absent now.

### The mockers’ script

> “All who see me mock me…  
> ‘He rolled himself onto YHWH — let Him deliver him!  
> Let Him rescue him, since He delights in him!’”  
> — Psalm 22:7–8, working rendering

These are the words Matthew places in the mouths around the cross.

The psalm is not only giving Yeshua language. It is giving Matthew language for the enemies.

### The body in the dust

The speaker describes bodily collapse, public exposure, encircling enemies, thirst, and death:

> “I am poured out like water…  
> my strength is dried like a potsherd,  
> and my tongue clings to my jaws;  
> you lay me in the dust of death.”  
> — Psalm 22:14–15

Then:

> “They divide my garments among themselves,  
> and for my clothing they cast lots.”  
> — Psalm 22:18

One famous line in verse 16 contains a genuine textual problem — “like a lion, my hands and feet” in the dominant Masoretic tradition versus forms behind ancient witnesses often rendered “they pierced” or “they dug.” That debate deserves its own excavation. This module does not need to force it. The Passion-Psalm connection is already visible without pretending an unsettled line is simple.

### The petition

The speaker continues:

> “But you, YHWH, do not be far away.  
> My strength, hurry to my help.”  
> — Psalm 22:19

The prayer does not treat the opening experience as the only possible reality. It asks the God who feels far not to be far.

### The turn

By verse 22, the voice has moved into proclamation:

> “I will declare your name to my brothers;  
> in the midst of the assembly I will praise you.”  
> — Psalm 22:22

Then the psalm gives its own interpretation of what happened:

> **“For He did not despise or detest the affliction of the afflicted one;  
> He did not hide His face from him;  
> when he cried to Him, He heard.”**  
> — Psalm 22:24, working rendering

Do not run past that sentence.

The psalm that begins, “Why have you forsaken me?” later says:

- He did not despise the afflicted one.
- He did not hide His face from him.
- He heard the cry.

That does not make the opening question dishonest.

It means the opening question is not the psalm’s final account of God.

### The horizon widens

The ending expands beyond one sufferer:

> “All the ends of the earth will remember and return to YHWH,  
> and all the families of the nations will worship before you.  
> For the kingdom belongs to YHWH,  
> and He rules among the nations.”  
> — Psalm 22:27–28, working rendering

A cry that begins with apparent abandonment ends with a congregation, the nations, the kingdom, and a coming generation hearing what God has done.

Do not use the ending to silence the beginning.

Do not use the beginning to erase the ending.

The psalm insists on both.

## Did Yeshua mean the whole psalm?

This is where honest readers divide.

Several possibilities remain live:

1. Yeshua uses the opening because it exactly expresses the anguish of the moment.
2. He identifies himself with Psalm 22’s afflicted speaker.
3. He deliberately invokes the whole prayer, including its movement toward hearing and praise.
4. The historical hearers may catch only the cry while the evangelist expects later readers to recover more.
5. The line can do all of those things at once.

What can we prove?

We can prove that the words quote Psalm 22:1.

We can show that the Passion narrative is already threaded with Psalm 22.

We can show that Yeshua’s Gospel speech repeatedly uses passages whose home contexts matter.

We can show that Christian Scripture later places another line from Psalm 22 in the Son’s mouth.

Hebrews says:

> “The one sanctifying and those being sanctified are all from One; for this reason he is not ashamed to call them brothers, saying:
>
> **‘I will proclaim your name to my brothers;  
> in the midst of the assembly I will sing your praise.’**”  
> — Hebrews 2:11–12, quoting Psalm 22:22

This is **steel within the Christian canon: direct quotation with explicit Christological attribution**.

Hebrews hears the speaker who suffered in Psalm 22 as the Son who stands among his brothers and praises God.

That does not prove what every person at Golgotha understood when Yeshua cried the first line.

It does prove that the New Testament itself does not confine Psalm 22’s relevance to verse 1.

So here is the honest grade:

### Direct quotation — steel

Yeshua’s cry is Psalm 22:1.

### Passion-scene saturation — strong allusion

Garments, head-shaking, trust-taunt, and cry place the crucifixion inside the psalm’s language-world.

### Whole-psalm invocation — strong literary/canonical inference

The opening line plausibly functions as a remez-like cue to the whole prayer, especially because the scene has already activated several of its lines and Hebrews later applies verse 22 to Christ.

### “Yeshua told everyone to recite the entire psalm” — theological synthesis

Beautiful, plausible, and not directly stated.

The difference between those grades is not an attempt to weaken the claim.

It is how we keep the claim strong enough to trust.

## Chosen or spontaneous is the wrong fight

We often imagine two possibilities:

Either Yeshua calmly selected Psalm 22 after a detached internal analysis,

or agony overwhelmed him and familiar words escaped without intention.

That is a false choice.

A person formed by Scripture can speak reflexively and intentionally at the same time.

What surfaces under pressure may be precisely what has been chosen over years of formation.

The Gospels present Yeshua’s speech as saturated with Scripture in public controversy, private teaching, temptation, prayer, and death. That is visible. We do not need the unverifiable claim that he had “prayed this psalm every day since childhood.”

The text gives us enough:

> Scripture is not an emergency substitute for Yeshua’s own words. Scripture is part of how he understands and performs his mission.

This is why **purpose does not erase suffering, and suffering does not erase purpose** is the better center.

The cry can be an eruption from genuine agony.

The cry can be an intentional scriptural act.

The cry can be both because the speaker has been formed deeply enough that his most immediate speech is also meaningful speech.

Formation is intentionality with a longer fuse.

---

## The other dying words

We need one more guardrail.

Matthew and Mark are not the only Gospel witnesses to Yeshua’s speech from the cross. Each Gospel shapes the Passion with its own emphases, and we should not mash them into one featureless transcript.

But a Christian canonical reading may compare them.

### Luke: another psalm

Luke records:

> “Father, into your hands I entrust my spirit.”  
> — Luke 23:46, echoing Psalm 31:5

Another dying word is scriptural prayer.

### John: Scripture named as purpose

John writes:

> “After this, Yeshua, knowing that everything had now been accomplished, so that the Scripture might be fulfilled, said, ‘I thirst.’”  
> — John 19:28, working rendering

John explicitly presents scriptural fulfillment as a category for speech from the cross.

That does not prove Matthew’s exact intention.

It proves the early Gospel tradition did not consider “genuine physical suffering” and “purposeful scriptural speech” to be competitors.

“I thirst” can be bodily truth and scriptural act.

So can Psalm 22.

### Gethsemane: the choice upstream

In Matthew, the willing obedience is established before the crucifixion:

> “My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me. Yet not as I will, but as you.”  
> — Matthew 26:39

The mission is not invented at the ninth hour.

The ninth hour is where the mission is endured.

### One precision note

Matthew 27:46 is not literally Yeshua’s final breath. Matthew 27:50 records another loud cry before he gives up his spirit, though Matthew does not preserve its words.

The weight of “Eli, Eli” rests on its specificity, placement, and scriptural home — not on the dramatic but inaccurate claim that he had only one breath left and spent it on this line.

## Five different claims hiding inside “forsaken”

Now we can return to the question people usually ask:

> **Did the Father abandon the Son?**

Before answering, ask what *abandon* means.

### 1. The Father did not rescue Yeshua from the cross

Yes.

The mockers demand rescue. None comes. The cup is not removed. Yeshua remains exposed to enemies and death.

This is plain narrative fact.

### 2. Yeshua entered genuine desolation

Yes — if by desolation we mean the human extremity voiced by Psalm 22:1.

The cry is not theater. The first line is not disposable packaging around a triumphant ending. Even if the whole psalm is active, Yeshua reaches its destination through the abyss, not around it.

What we should not do is turn the cry into a clinical report about every proposition present in his human consciousness. The Gospel gives his words, not a narrated interior monologue.

### 3. Yeshua bore judgment and sin within his mission

This is a Christian theological claim with substantial canonical support.

Matthew says his life is a ransom for many and his blood is poured out for forgiveness. The cup and the darkness carry judgment associations. The wider New Testament speaks of curse, sin-bearing, sacrifice, and reconciliation.

But none of that is encoded mechanically in the tense of one verb.

Atonement belongs in the argument.

It cannot be smuggled in as grammar.

### 4. The Father repudiated the Son or ceased to love him

The cry by itself does not establish this.

Psalm 22’s own destination says God did not despise the afflicted one, did not hide His face, and heard the cry.

The mission narratives present Father and Son as united in purpose. In John, Yeshua says that the disciples will leave him alone, **“yet I am not alone, because the Father is with me”** (John 16:32). Luke’s dying prayer addresses **Father** and entrusts his spirit into the Father’s hands.

Those canonical witnesses do not remove Matthew’s darkness.

They do prevent us from turning darkness into filial repudiation without argument.

### 5. The Father and Son were metaphysically separated

The text does not say this.

Classical Christian confession cannot speak as though the divine life fractured, the Trinity temporarily became two, or the Father ceased to be Father toward the eternal Son.

A Christian may speak of judicial forsakenness, withdrawal of experienced consolation, nonintervention, curse-bearing, or the full human experience of dereliction. Those proposals need to be defined and argued.

But **metaphysical rupture** is not a synonym for “why have you forsaken me.”

One English word has been carrying too many suitcases.

Set them down and open them one at a time.

---

## Did Yeshua think the Father had abandoned him?

No text gives us uncomplicated access to that interior question.

That is not an escape. It is a boundary.

Matthew does not write:

> “At that moment Yeshua concluded that the Father had ceased to be present.”

Nor does Matthew write:

> “Yeshua felt no abandonment at all and spoke only to teach the crowd.”

Matthew gives us something more demanding than either simplification:

- Yeshua genuinely cries the language of forsakenness.
- He still says **“my God”** twice.
- He chooses — or, at minimum, Matthew deliberately gives him — the opening of a specific psalm.
- The Passion scene has already activated that psalm.
- The psalm’s own final interpretation says God did not hide His face and heard the afflicted one.
- The mission has been knowingly embraced before the cross.

So the safest conclusion is not:

> “Yeshua mistakenly believed the Father was gone.”

And it is not:

> “Yeshua was merely signaling victory and did not mean the question.”

It is:

> **Yeshua voices genuine dereliction by placing the moment inside a prayer whose destination is hearing, praise, and the reign of God.**

He does not give us one closed proposition about abandonment.

He tells us where the answer must be sought.

He puts the event inside Psalm 22.

---

## The doctrine people often import

A common Christian explanation says:

> “God is too holy to look at sin, so when Yeshua bore sin, the Father turned His face away.”

That sentence may be intended as a metaphor for judgment.

But it is not what Matthew says.

The verse often used to support “God cannot look at sin,” Habakkuk 1:13, is itself part of a complaint asking why God *does* look upon treachery without immediately acting. It is not a metaphysical statement that God is unable to perceive evil.

And Psalm 22:24 says, in the very prayer Yeshua names:

> **“He did not hide His face from him.”**

That does not settle every theory of atonement. It does mean the “turned face” slogan cannot be presented as though it were Psalm 22’s own explanation.

The cross truly judges sin.

The Son truly bears what he came to bear.

The Father truly does not remove the cup.

But Christian theology should describe the cross as God’s act of reconciliation, not as the Father becoming the Son’s enemy in the way sinners are enemies.

Paul says:

> “God was in Christ reconciling the world to Himself.”  
> — 2 Corinthians 5:19, common rendering of the sense

Hebrews says Christ offered himself **through the eternal Spirit** to God (Hebrews 9:14).

The cross is not a fracture in God’s life.

It is the terrible, unified act by which God enters and overcomes the alienation of sin.

That is theological synthesis.

I state it as Christian conviction, not as a hidden definition of an Aramaic verb.

## Remez: “Recite the whole thing”

Now return to the possibility that started this excavation.

The mockers have already quoted Psalm 22.

Yeshua answers with its opening line.

Could the cry mean, in effect:

> *You are speaking the psalm without knowing it. Here is its beginning. Go read the whole thing.*

That is a compelling reading.

It explains why the opening line matters in a scene already crowded with the psalm.

It fits Yeshua’s established use of context-bearing Scripture.

It fits the Gospel tradition’s presentation of scriptural speech at death.

It fits Hebrews’ later attribution of Psalm 22:22 to the Son.

And it makes sense of the strange power the line still has: the cry does not end thought; it opens a text.

But keep the metal labeled.

### What is steel

- Yeshua quotes Psalm 22:1.
- Matthew’s Passion contains several close Psalm 22 correspondences.
- The mockers’ trust-taunt strongly echoes Psalm 22:8.
- Hebrews applies Psalm 22:22 to the Son.

### What is strong inference

- The opening line functions as an invitation to interpret the scene through the larger psalm.
- Matthew’s wilderness and cross scenes form a Son-of-God testing pattern.
- Yeshua’s scriptural use characteristically depends on source context.

### What is theological synthesis

- Yeshua deliberately gives the opening line as a remez meaning, “recite the psalm in full.”
- He consciously intends every hearer to activate every movement of the psalm.
- Each Passion detail is a one-to-one predictive code written solely for the crucifixion.

The synthesis can be right without becoming steel.

The best argument does not need a false badge.

## The answer hidden in the refusal to come down

The crowd thinks rescue would prove sonship.

Matthew has already taught the reader the opposite.

In the wilderness, throwing himself down would not have proved trust. It would have been testing God.

At the cross, coming down would not have proved mission. It would have abandoned it.

The taunt says:

> “If you are the Son of God, save yourself.”

The Gospel’s answer is:

> **Because he is the Son, he does not save himself from the work by which he saves others.**

That sentence is theological synthesis, but Matthew gives it strong narrative support:

> “He saved others; he cannot save himself.”  
> — Matthew 27:42

The mockers mean contradiction.

The Gospel hears irony.

The inability is not lack of power. It is the moral necessity of love, obedience, and mission. He cannot save himself *and* complete the work in the way he has chosen to complete it.

Then the mockers quote Psalm 22’s demand for rescue.

Yeshua gives Psalm 22’s cry.

Not because purpose has vanished.

Because purpose has reached the place where obedience hurts.

Purpose does not erase suffering.

Suffering does not erase purpose.

The cross holds both until neither can be reduced to the other.

## Where the text aims

If you come to this study as an outsider, you do not have to accept Christian theology to see the literary architecture.

A condemned man hangs in a scene narrated through an ancient psalm. His enemies unknowingly speak that psalm at him. Then he gives its first line aloud. The Gospel has previously shown him answering an “if you are the Son” test with context-bearing Scripture, and the same test returns at his death.

At minimum, this is more than random verbal debris.

It is a serious ancient claim about how the death should be read.

If you come as a believer, the warning cuts in two directions.

Do not protect Christology by making the suffering theatrical.

And do not protect the suffering by stripping the speaker of identity and mission.

The appalling reality is not that Yeshua forgot the mission.

It is that the mission went through this.

His life is not only a rescue accomplished for us. It is a tutorial in how truth is inhabited under pressure. In the wilderness, in controversy, in prayer, and at the cross, Scripture is not ammunition detached from context. It is the world in which he recognizes what faithfulness requires.

That changes the practice.

The lesson is not merely:

> “When your own words run out, borrow a verse.”

Maybe his words did not run out.

Maybe these were exactly the words.

A better practice is:

> **Let Scripture form you before the darkness, so that when pressure strips away performance, what remains is language deep enough to hold both the wound and the destination.**

And one final caution:

Do not use Yeshua’s mission to tell every suffering person that every wound was specifically assigned as necessary cargo.

The text tells us what his cross means.

It does not give us permission to explain every person’s trauma from above.

What it does give sufferers is a prayer large enough to say:

> “Why have you forsaken me?”

without requiring that the experience become the final truth about God.

## The shovel: how to read a cry that quotes Scripture

This study’s tool is **context recovery under pressure**.

Here is how to use it.

### Step one: identify what is actually explicit

What words are quoted?

Is there a citation formula?

Is the source identifiable?

Here, the source is Psalm 22:1. Steel.

### Step two: separate the cry from the conclusions built on it

Write the propositions separately:

- suffering,
- nonrescue,
- experienced absence,
- judicial judgment,
- relational repudiation,
- metaphysical separation.

Do not let one word prove all six.

### Step three: go home with the quotation

Read the whole source passage.

What does it say before and after the quoted line?

What tension does the source itself hold?

Here, Psalm 22 moves from forsakenness-language to the declaration that God did not hide His face and heard.

### Step four: inspect the new scene for other traces of the source

Are there repeated images, words, gestures, or arguments?

Here: garments, head-shaking, trust-taunt, cry.

### Step five: compare the speaker’s established use of Scripture

Does this speaker habitually use isolated slogans, or context-bearing passages?

Yeshua’s wilderness citations make their strongest sense from Deuteronomy’s larger wilderness setting.

### Step six: distinguish history from literary presentation

What can be said about what the evangelist is doing?

What can be said about Yeshua’s interior consciousness?

Those are not always the same claim.

### Step seven: grade the metal

- **Direct quotation**
- **Strong allusion**
- **Shared motif**
- **Literary synthesis**
- **Canonical theological synthesis**
- **Doctrinal interpretation**
- **Speculation**

Do not invent a flattering tier because you love the conclusion.

### Step eight: audit salience

Before asking, “Is this defensible?” ask:

> **Why are we making this the statement at all?**

A true statement can still distort a short reading if it becomes the controlling focus and pushes equally necessary truths out of view.

That is how a card about genuine suffering became a doctrine of abandonment without noticing the border it crossed.

## The chain, standing back

- **Psalm 22:1** — the direct source of the cry.
- **Psalm 22:7–8, 18** — mockery, head-shaking, trust-taunt, divided garments.
- **Matthew 27:35, 39, 43, 46** — the Passion narrated inside that psalm’s language.
- **Matthew 4:3–11** — the earlier “if you are the Son” Scripture-test, answered from Deuteronomy’s wilderness context.
- **Matthew 26:36–46** — the cup faced and the mission willingly embraced before Golgotha.
- **Luke 23:46 / Psalm 31:5** — another dying prayer spoken from Scripture.
- **John 19:28** — a Gospel explicitly linking speech from the cross with Scripture’s fulfillment.
- **Psalm 22:22 / Hebrews 2:12** — the afflicted psalmist’s praise placed directly in the Son’s mouth.
- **Psalm 22:24** — the psalm’s own declaration that God did not hide His face and heard.
- **Psalm 22:27–28** — the ends of the earth, the nations, and YHWH’s kingdom.

The cry is not a closed answer.

It is a deliberate interpretive address:

> **Read what is happening here through this prayer.**

And do not skip either its abyss or its destination.

## A prayer

Father,

Keep me from making the cross easier than it was.

Do not let me use purpose to numb the suffering, or suffering to erase the purpose.

Teach me to hear Yeshua’s cry without forcing one word to say more than it says. When he says, “Why have you forsaken me?” keep me from turning anguish into theater — and keep me from turning anguish into a fracture in You.

Let me go home with the quotation.

Let me hear the mockers speaking a psalm they do not know they are fulfilling. Let me see the Son refusing, again, to prove himself by self-rescue. Let me read until the prayer that begins with distance reaches the sentence: You did not hide Your face. You heard.

Form Your Word in me before the dark.

Not as a shelf of slogans, but as truth deep enough to rise when everything shallow has been stripped away.

And when I cannot reconcile the wound with the destination, give me the honesty to say “why,” the relationship to keep saying “my God,” and the patience not to make my present experience the final definition of who You are.

For the glory of the Father, through Yeshua the Son, by the Spirit who carried him through the offering.

Amen.

## Research notes for council review

### Publication posture

This is a Christian module, not a neutralized comparative-religion essay. Its governing commitments are:

- Yeshua is fully human and not merely human.
- His suffering is genuine.
- His mission and identity remain interpretively relevant at the cross.
- Linguistic or historical evidence may not be distorted to protect doctrine.
- Theology must be labeled as theology rather than smuggled in as grammar.
- Jewish Scripture must first be represented honestly in its own literary world.
- Non-Christian readers are invited to examine the argument without being asked to pretend they already share its premises.

### Claims that should survive review

1. **Matthew 27:46 / Mark 15:34 directly quote Psalm 22:1 in translated sense.**
2. **Matthew 27 contains multiple strong Psalm 22 correspondences before the cry.**
3. **Matthew 27:43 strongly echoes Psalm 22:8; the mockers invoke the psalm before Yeshua gives its opening line.**
4. **Matthew 4 and 27 repeat the “if you are the Son of God” challenge and the pressure toward demonstrative self-rescue.**
5. **Yeshua’s Deuteronomy citations in Matthew 4 draw their force from Israel’s wilderness context.**
6. **Hebrews 2:12 directly attributes Psalm 22:22 to the Son.**
7. **Psalm 22:24 says God did not hide His face from the afflicted one and heard the cry.**
8. **John 19:28 presents scriptural fulfillment as part of its framing of a saying from the cross.**
9. **Matthew 27:46 is not literally Yeshua’s final breath; Matthew 27:50 records another loud cry without words.**

### Claims that must remain graded

- **The whole psalm is intentionally invoked:** strong literary/canonical inference.
- **The cry functions as remez meaning “recite in full”:** theological synthesis.
- **Matthew intentionally constructs the cross as the return of the wilderness temptation:** strong literary synthesis.
- **Yeshua’s subjective experience included felt abandonment:** plausible reading of the cry, not direct access to interior psychology.
- **Judicial forsakenness as an atonement category:** Christian theological interpretation from the wider canon.
- **Relational or metaphysical separation in the Trinity:** not established by the text and outside classical Christian bounds if taken as an actual fracture in divine being.

### Historical-language cautions

- Matthew’s *Eli* and Mark’s *Eloi* differ; the exact historical utterance cannot be reconstructed simply from either Greek transliteration.
- *Sabachthani* reflects an Aramaic root; Psalm 22’s Hebrew has *azavtani*.
- Do not use the Hebrew qatal or Greek aorist to prove finality, duration, or metaphysical separation.
- Use *remez* descriptively, not as proof that a later formal PaRDeS system governed the event.
- Do not call Matthew 27:46 Yeshua’s “last breath.”
- Do not claim Psalm 22:31, “he has done it,” is a direct quotation behind John’s “It is finished” without additional evidence.
- Do not build the module on the disputed “pierced / like a lion” reading of Psalm 22:16.

### Suggested scholarly checks

- Richard B. Hays, *Echoes of Scripture in the Gospels* — metalepsis and Gospel scriptural echoes.
- Jason Robert Combs, “An Extended Inverted Allusion to Psalm 22 in Mark 15” — the reverse-order Psalm 22 proposal in Mark’s Passion.
- Daniel M. Gurtner, *The Torn Veil: Matthew’s Exposition of the Death of Jesus* — Matthew’s Passion theology and literary structure.
- S. van Tilborg, “Language, Meaning, Sense and Reference: Matthew’s Passion Narrative and Psalm 22” — Psalm 22 as an anchor for Matthew’s Passion narration.
- Henk Jan de Jonge, “The Reading Asabthani in Luther’s Translation of Matthew 27.46” — Hebrew/Aramaic forms behind the cry.
- Bryan R. Dyer, “‘In the Midst of the Assembly I Will Praise You’: Hebrews 2.12 and Its Contribution to the Argument of the Epistle” — Psalm 22:22 in Hebrews.
- Jewish reception of Psalm 22 should be checked independently so the module does not treat its Christian canonical reuse as the psalm’s only legitimate reading.
