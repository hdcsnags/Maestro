## Review output
This module is one of the most carefully calibrated pieces of Passion exegesis I've read, and most of its caution is real work, not performance. But there are two or three places where the hedging machinery keeps running after the evidence has already paid out — and one minor spot where a grade drifts high. Let me walk through both directions.

## Where the caution genuinely serves the reader

These are the load-bearing hedges, and they earn their keep:

- **The grammar firewall.** "The Hebrew *azavtani* is a qatal form. Matthew's Greek translation *enkatelipes* is an aorist. Neither fact proves that the abandonment was final, metaphysical, relational, temporary, perceived, judicial, or complete." This is exactly right, and it's a real error in circulation — people do preach tense-forms as Trinitarian metaphysics. Same for the refusal to build on the disputed "pierced / like a lion" reading of Psalm 22:16, and the honest note that *Eli/Eloi* are Greek transliterations, not audio.

- **The five-suitcase unpacking of "forsaken."** Distinguishing nonrescue, desolation, sin-bearing, repudiation, and metaphysical separation as *different propositions* is the module's best move. It correctly grants #1 and #2 confidently, locates #3 as canon-wide theology rather than verb-tense magic, and blocks #5 without dismissing every orthodox account of dereliction. Crucially, it does **not** let one tradition's formulation become "the Christian reading" — it names "judicial forsakenness, withdrawal of experienced consolation, nonintervention, curse-bearing" as live options needing argument, rather than crowning or anathematizing any of them. That's the discipline done right.

- **The Habakkuk 1:13 correction.** The observation that the "God cannot look at sin" prooftext sits inside a complaint asking why God *does* look on treachery is textually accurate, and using Psalm 22:24 ("He did not hide His face from him") to say the turned-face slogan "cannot be presented as though it were Psalm 22's own explanation" is precisely scoped — it's a claim about the psalm's content, not an illicit leap to Golgotha.

- **The PaRDeS warning.** Refusing to claim Matthew 27 documents a formally named first-century *remez* rule ("history by enthusiasm") while keeping the descriptive insight is exactly how to handle anachronism.

- **Hebrews 2:12 graded as steel.** Correct — that is direct quotation with explicit Christological attribution, and the module rightly notes it proves the NT itself doesn't confine Psalm 22's relevance to verse 1.

## Where the caution performs rather than serves

**1. "He chooses — or, at minimum, Matthew deliberately gives him — the opening of a specific psalm."**

This is the clearest flinch. The module has spent thousands of words assembling the evidence for intentional speech: Yeshua's habitual context-bearing use of Deuteronomy under the *identical* "if you are the Son of God" pressure; repeated passion-and-resurrection predictions; Gethsemane's consent placed "on the record before the nails"; Luke's dying word being *another* psalm; John explicitly presenting scriptural fulfillment as a category for speech from the cross. Then, at the moment of conclusion, it retreats to a fifty-fifty hedge between "he chose" and "Matthew gave him." Within the app's own confessed frame — where the Gospels are testimony to what happened, not free composition — that hedge is not neutral. A body of established, consistent characterization *is* evidence of intent, the same way a novelist's chapter-twenty callback doesn't require telepathy to call deliberate. Nobody needs access to interior consciousness to say "he chose these words"; the module's own section "Chosen or spontaneous is the wrong fight" argues exactly this ("formation is intentionality with a longer fuse") — and then the hedge un-says it.

**2. "It tells us how Matthew and Mark ask their readers to hear the event. That is the level of evidence we actually possess."**

This sentence quietly imports the redaction-critical default: that saturation evidence reaches only the *narration*, never the *event*. But the module elsewhere treats the mockers' words as things actually said ("The mockers quote the psalm before Yeshua does" — stated flatly, and listed in the research notes as a claim that "should survive review"). Inside a frame that confesses these narratives as true witness, "the level of evidence we actually possess" includes testimony about the hill, not just about the evangelists' desks. The module can't have it both ways, and its own confessed posture licenses the stronger way.

**3. The wilderness/cross parallel graded as "strong literary-theological synthesis."**

Undergraded. "If you are the Son of God" is *verbatim* repetition within a single author's composition (Matthew 4:3, 6; 27:40), combined with Scripture-wielded-as-pressure and the demand for demonstrative rescue in both scenes. The module's defense of the low grade — "Matthew never inserts a narrator's note" — proves too much; authors almost never do, and verbatim repeated challenge-formulas within one book are how ancient narrative *makes* narrator's notes. That Matthew designed the cross as the temptation's return is closer to strong allusion than to synthesis. (Whether *Yeshua at the cross* intended the reprise is a separate, properly softer claim — the module could have split those two grades and been more confident on the first.)

**4. Minor overclaim, for symmetry.** Under "What is steel," the module lists "The mockers' trust-taunt strongly echoes Psalm 22:8." Steel is defined earlier as *direct quotation*; Matthew 27:43 is a close allusion (with Wisdom of Solomon 2 also plausibly in the mix), not a citation — and the module's own wording ("strongly echoes") betrays the mismatch. It belongs one shelf down, where the module already put the garments and head-shaking. Also small: "They do not know they are doing it" states the mockers' ignorance of the echo as flat narrative fact; it's an excellent inference from Matthean irony, but it's an inference.

## The verdict

The module's grading system is its glory, and the labels are almost all honest — theology stated as conviction, synthesis labeled as synthesis, the human agony never theatricalized. Where it fails, it fails in the direction its own council notes worried about least: not overclaiming, but hedging past the point where its confessed frame and its own assembled evidence had already carried the claim. The fear of saying "Yeshua meant this" quietly re-opens, twice, a question the whole recorded shape of his speech — which this very study documents — had closed.

CANONICAL CALIBRATION

Overclaimed:
Matthew 27:43 listed under "steel" when the module's own definition reserves steel for direct quotation and its own wording ("strongly echoes") concedes it is allusion; and the mockers' unawareness of quoting Psalm 22 stated as flat fact rather than (excellent) inference. Both minor; neither affects the argument's spine.

Underclaimed:
(1) "He chooses — or, at minimum, Matthew deliberately gives him" — the fifty-fifty hedge on Yeshua's intentionality, after the module itself assembled the characterization evidence (habitual context-bearing citation under the same "if you are the Son" test, predicted passion, Gethsemane consent, Luke 23:46, John 19:28) that licenses "he chose these words" without any claim to interior consciousness. (2) "That is the level of evidence we actually possess" — restricting the Psalm 22 saturation to evangelist presentation only, contradicting the module's own flat claim that the mockers spoke first, and importing a narration-only default the app's confessed frame does not hold. (3) The wilderness/cross parallel graded "synthesis" despite verbatim intra-Matthean repetition of the challenge formula — Matthew's design is strong allusion territory.
