# My reaction, reading as someone still new to this

I sat with this one longer than I expected to, because it's about the one line from the cross that always stopped me cold — "why have you forsaken me?" I've heard that verse quoted my whole short time as a Christian like it's the moment something broke between God and Jesus. So I came in ready to either get that confirmed or get it complicated. It got complicated, in a way I think I needed.

## What actually landed

The thing that struck me hardest was this: **the people mocking Jesus were already quoting the psalm before he said anything.** "They divide his clothes... they shake their heads... 'he trusted in God, let God rescue him now.'" I did not know that was Psalm 22 being acted out by the crowd first. That reframed the whole scene for me — it's not just a man in pain reaching for whatever words were nearby. Something bigger is already running underneath the moment before he ever opens his mouth. I'd repeat that detail to a friend before anything else in this study.

The second thing I'd remember: the study insists on separating "Jesus suffered" from "the Father actually turned away from him" — and shows its work. It quotes Psalm 22:24 directly: *"He did not despise or detest the affliction of the afflicted one; He did not hide His face from him; when he cried to Him, He heard."* That's inside the very prayer Jesus is quoting. That genuinely surprised me — I always assumed the psalm just started sad and stayed sad until it turned into a "but God is still good" ending tacked on. Finding out the abandonment-question and the "he heard me" answer are both spoken by the same voice, in the same prayer, made the cry feel less like a crack in the story and more like the whole story compressed into one line.

## What felt like good news vs. what felt like a rule

The good news, plainly: I'm allowed to say "why have you forsaken me" in my own life without that being the final truth about God, and without anyone telling me I lack faith for saying it. The study says this explicitly — that a "why" doesn't have to become "the final definition of who You are." That's the part I'd actually carry into a hard week.

The part that felt more like a rule, or at least a fence going up: the long section warning me *not* to conclude "the Father repudiated the Son" or "the Trinity fractured." I believe the writer is right to draw that line, and they show real work for it (Psalm 22:24, John 16:32, Luke 23:46). But I noticed I was told fairly firmly what conclusion *not* to draw, right after being shown very moving evidence that could easily be read that way. It felt less like "here's an open question" and more like "here's the wrong answer, don't go there" — even while the study was praising itself for not doing exactly that kind of closing-down. I don't think that's dishonest, but it is the one spot where I felt steered rather than just shown.

## The place I'd flag as answering a different question

The section "Did Yeshua think the Father had abandoned him?" promises not to resolve it — and then the very next lines give me a tightly worded formula anyway: *"Yeshua voices genuine dereliction by placing the moment inside a prayer whose destination is hearing, praise, and the reign of God."* That sentence reads to me like an answer wearing the costume of a non-answer. I went in wondering "did God actually leave him, even for a second," and I came out with a beautifully built sentence that technically declines to say yes or no — but functions, in practice, as "no, not really, because the psalm has a happy ending." I'd want a friend to notice that the door was propped as "still open" but the room behind it was already furnished.

## What I would tell a friend this verse/study means

**Jesus really did cry out feeling forsaken by God, and that cry wasn't a script he broke character to deliver — it was the opening line of a prayer he was living inside that ends with God never having hidden His face and having heard him all along, so when I feel forsaken I can say so honestly, without believing that feeling is the last word on whether God is with me.**
