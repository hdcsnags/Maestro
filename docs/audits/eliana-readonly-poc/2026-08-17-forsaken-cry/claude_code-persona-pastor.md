## Reading "The Forsaken Cry"

I read this the way I read anything before I'd hand it to someone in a pew: not "is this true," but "who's sitting in front of me when this word lands, and what does it do to them."

### What actually stopped me

The line that made me put the study down for a second: *"Do not use Yeshua's mission to tell every suffering person that every wound was specifically assigned as necessary cargo."* That sentence is doing real pastoral labor. I have sat with people who were told, by well-meaning elders, that their miscarriage or their cancer or their abuse was "part of God's plan" the way the cross was — and watched what that does to a person's face. Most teaching on the cross never even notices it's making that leap. This one catches itself doing it and steps back. That's rare, and I'd repeat it verbatim to a small group leader before they touched this passage.

The other moment I'd actually carry out of the room is the unpacking of "forsaken" into its six separate suitcases — suffering, nonrescue, desolation, judgment, repudiation, metaphysical rupture — and the insistence that *"One English word has been carrying too many suitcases. Set them down and open them one at a time."* That is genuinely useful to someone mid-crisis who is saying "God has abandoned me" and doesn't know which of those six things they mean. A skilled counselor could use that list at a hospital bedside to help someone locate their own grief instead of drowning in the word. That's the study doing hard, honest work rather than borrowed comfort.

And I want to name the discipline of refusing the cheap version of atonement theology — *"God is too holy to look at sin, so when Yeshua bore sin, the Father turned His face away"* — and then actually checking it against Habakkuk 1:13 and Psalm 22:24 ("He did not hide His face from him") and finding the slogan doesn't hold up. Most congregations have heard that slogan preached as gospel truth. Correcting it costs the teacher something, because it's the easier sermon. This one didn't take the easier sermon.

### What worried me

Here's my problem: this is built like a peer-reviewed footnote and delivered like a sermon. The apparatus — "steel," "strong inference," "theological synthesis," Hebrew qatal versus Greek aorist, Combs and Gurtner and van Tilborg in the reference list — is exactly right for a person who distrusts pat answers and wants to see the evidence graded before they'll believe the conclusion. It is exactly wrong for a person who is currently *in* Psalm 22:1 and needs someone in the room with them, not an epistemology lecture. If I read this aloud on a Sunday to a widow six weeks out from her husband's death, she is going to sit through three thousand words of "we cannot prove Yeshua's interior consciousness" before she reaches "He did not hide His face... He heard" — and by then I've made her wait through my hedging to get the one sentence she came for.

The sharper risk is this passage: *"He cannot save himself and complete the work in the way he has chosen to complete it... The inability is not lack of power. It is the moral necessity of love, obedience, and mission."* Read in isolation — lifted out, the way a phrase gets lifted out in a bad marriage counseling session — that is the exact shape of the sentence someone uses on a woman who wants to leave: *your endurance is the proof of your faithfulness; leaving is refusing the mission.* The study does eventually forbid that use, but the guardrail sentence about "necessary cargo" arrives paragraphs later, decoupled from the moment of danger, in a different section entirely. A careful teacher would fuse those two sentences together every time they're spoken aloud. Nothing in the text forces that fusion to happen.

### Who this was actually written for

**Helped:** the doubting teenager, or the adult who's quietly stopped believing but keeps showing up. Someone who has been fed "God turned His face away" and "he was signaling victory the whole time" as if those were settled facts will recognize, with relief, a teacher willing to say *"That would be history by enthusiasm"* about their own tradition's overreach. This reader gets treated as capable of holding an unresolved question, and that respect is itself the pastoral care.

**At risk:** the person in the abusive situation being told to honor the wrong one — specifically if she meets this alone, on a screen, without the person who wrote it in the room to attach the guardrail to the danger sentence in real time. She could stop reading exactly where the "moral necessity of obedience" line sits, before reaching the later paragraph that forbids using it on her.

Weighing the whole thing — the density, the citations, the insistence on grading every claim before it's allowed to comfort anyone — I think this was written for the skeptical, textually literate reader who needs to be persuaded the teacher isn't cheating, more than it was written to hold a suffering person's hand. It's honest work. But if I used it Sunday morning, I would not let it stand alone — I'd need to sit with that widow afterward myself, because the text gives her the comfort, but only after making her wait through the argument to earn it.
