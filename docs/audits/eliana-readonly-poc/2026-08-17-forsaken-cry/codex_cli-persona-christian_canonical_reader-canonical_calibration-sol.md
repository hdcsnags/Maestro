## Review output
The module is largely well calibrated. It handles the manuscript, grammatical, literary, and doctrinal distinctions unusually carefully without denying either Yeshua’s real agony or the canonical shape of his mission. Its main calibration problem is not skepticism about the Psalm 22 connection; it is that the text sometimes establishes intentionality convincingly and then retreats as though intentionality required direct access to Yeshua’s private consciousness.

The strongest work is its separation of propositions hidden inside “forsaken.” “Not rescued from the cross,” “genuine desolation,” “bearing sin and judgment,” “filial repudiation,” and “metaphysical separation” are rightly distinguished. Neither the Hebrew qatal nor Matthew’s aorist decides those theological questions. The caution about *remez* and the later PaRDeS scheme is also real historical discipline, as is the refusal to build the argument on Psalm 22:16’s disputed reading. Likewise, “purpose does not erase suffering, and suffering does not erase purpose” accurately protects both the Passion’s human reality and its confessed Christological meaning.

The module also correctly labels the wilderness–cross parallel “strong literary-theological synthesis.” Matthew never explicitly announces that structure, but “If you are the Son,” pressure toward spectacular rescue, Scripture used in the test, and Yeshua’s refusal to establish sonship on the adversary’s terms constitute substantial narrative evidence. This is neither plain textual fact nor imaginative free association.

There are, however, two places where it says more than the evidence permits.

First, “They do not know they are doing it. That is Matthew’s irony” moves from a strong claim about Matthew’s literary presentation to an unsupported claim about the mockers’ historical awareness. Matthew places Psalm 22:8 language in their mouths; that creates irony for the reader. Whether the historical speakers consciously recognized the psalm is not narrated. “They weaponize the psalm’s language … unaware” is plausible dramatic reconstruction, not established textual fact.

Second, the final chain declares:

> “It is a deliberate interpretive address: ‘Read what is happening here through this prayer.’”

That is probably right, but it is stronger than the module’s own grading. Earlier it properly calls whole-psalm invocation a “strong literary/canonical inference” and the paraphrase “recite the psalm in full” theological synthesis. The conclusion should preserve that distinction: the cry deliberately identifies Psalm 22 as the interpretive world of the event; the precise implied imperative “read the whole prayer” remains a compelling reconstruction of how that address works.

The larger problem runs in the opposite direction. The module says:

> “He chooses — or, at minimum, Matthew deliberately gives him — the opening of a specific psalm.”

That fallback is too weak inside the module’s confessed frame. It quietly substitutes a redactional minimum for the canonical claim being made. Matthew and Mark both present Yeshua as actually uttering the line. The Gospels consistently portray him as purposefully inhabiting Scripture; he predicts his Passion, interprets his death beforehand, answers decisive moments through context-bearing Scripture, and elsewhere prays Scripture while dying. The scene itself is saturated with Psalm 22 before he voices its opening. Within this canonical portrait, the responsible conclusion is that Yeshua intentionally invokes Psalm 22. One need not know whether the line arose through conscious selection at that second, long formation, immediate prayer, or all three. Those are questions about interior mechanism. Intentional public action can be inferred from established character, context, and conduct without possessing an interior transcript.

For the same reason, the five “live possibilities” are not equally live. “He deliberately invokes the whole prayer, including its movement toward hearing and praise” remains inferential; “he uses the opening because it exactly expresses anguish” is also plausible. But the suggestion that this might amount merely to borrowed anguish, with no purposeful scriptural identification by Yeshua, has markedly less canonical support than the module’s open list suggests. The text should distinguish uncertainty about the full scope of the invocation from uncertainty about whether the quotation is purposeful at all.

A similar underclaim appears in the treatment of filial repudiation. Saying:

> “The cry by itself does not establish this”

is true but unnecessarily narrow. The Christian-canonical question is not settled by the cry “by itself.” Psalm 22’s destination, Yeshua’s continued address to “my God,” his willing mission, John’s “the Father is with me,” Luke’s final entrustment to the Father, the resurrection, and the New Testament’s presentation of the cross as the triune work of reconciliation together license a positive conclusion: the Father did not cease to love or repudiate the Son. Precisely what “judicial forsakenness” means remains doctrinally contested, but actual filial rejection is not merely unproven by one verse; it is inconsistent with the module’s wider canonical evidence. The later language about the cross as a “terrible, unified act” reaches this conclusion more confidently, but the principal answer should do so too.
