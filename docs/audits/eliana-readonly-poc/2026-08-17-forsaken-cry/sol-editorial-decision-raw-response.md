## Decision

The document should be revised. Both reports identify the same central defect: the study accumulates substantial evidence for intentional scriptural action and then retreats to a redactional minimum. That convergence holds weight, and the underlying evidence independently supports it.

The clearest example occurs in “Did Yeshua think the Father had abandoned him?”:

> “He chooses — or, at minimum, Matthew deliberately gives him — the opening of a specific psalm.”

That alternative is not properly balanced. Matthew and Mark testify that Yeshua spoke the words. Within the document’s stated Christian and canonical posture, the responsible judgment is that he purposefully invoked Psalm 22. What remains inaccessible is the precise psychological mechanism and the exhaustive scope of his intention—not whether the scriptural act was purposeful.

Likewise, “The psalm was already in the air” ends by saying the correspondences tell us only how Matthew and Mark ask readers to hear the event. That is too restrictive. The evangelists certainly shape their narration, but they also testify that garments were divided, heads were shaken, the trust-taunt was spoken, and Yeshua cried the psalm’s opening. Literary presentation and historical testimony must be distinguished without treating them as competitors.

On the wilderness–cross parallel, Fable’s criticism is stronger than my earlier calibration. Matthew 4:3, 6 and 27:40 repeat the distinctive challenge “If you are the Son of God,” while both scenes pressure the Son to validate his identity through spectacular rescue. Scripture is enlisted in the test in both places. That is strong intra-Matthean allusion or narrative reprise, not merely a free-standing theological synthesis. The further claim that Yeshua consciously selected Psalm 22 specifically to answer the returning temptation remains an inference and should be labeled separately.

The minor overclaims also hold. In “What is steel,” Matthew 27:43 cannot be steel under the document’s own definition because it is not a formally marked quotation. It is a very close Psalm 22:8 allusion, possibly participating in a wider scriptural field. And “They do not know they are doing it” claims knowledge of the mockers’ subjective awareness that Matthew never supplies. The irony is textual; their ignorance is inferred.

My earlier objection to the final sentence also holds, but only in part. The evidence supports saying that Yeshua deliberately identifies the event with Psalm 22. It does not establish as direct fact the precise paraphrase, “Read the entire prayer and activate every movement.” The revision therefore affirms intentional invocation while retaining a grade for whole-psalm retrieval.

Finally, the original answer on filial repudiation is too weak. “The cry by itself does not establish this” is true but insufficient because the document is explicitly conducting a canonical Christian reading. Psalm 22:24, Yeshua’s continued address to “my God,” the embraced mission, John 16:32, Luke 23:46, the resurrection, and the New Testament’s presentation of the cross as God’s reconciling action support a positive conclusion: the Father did not cease to love or repudiate the Son. This does not decide every account of judicial forsakenness or experienced dereliction, but actual filial rejection is inconsistent with the canonical evidence assembled here.

## The sentence that became an answer before it became a question

Few sentences in Scripture have been made to carry more doctrine than this one.

Some hear it and say:

> *This is the moment the Father turned away from the Son.*

Others say:

> *This is the human Yeshua collapsing into the belief that God had abandoned him.*

Still others say:

> *This is not really anguish at all. It is a coded announcement that the psalm ends in victory.*

All three readings begin by trying to answer the sentence.

I want to begin one step earlier.

Why these words?

Yeshua has Scripture enough for every kind of pain. Psalm 31 gives him a prayer of trust. Psalm 69 gives him the voice of the persecuted. Psalm 88 ends in darkness. Job gives him page after page of protest. Yet Matthew and Mark preserve this cry:

> **Ἠλί Ἠλί λεμὰ σαβαχθανί;**
>
> *Ēli, Ēli, lema sabachthani?*
>
> **“My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”**  
> — Matthew 27:46

The sentence does not float alone over Golgotha.

Before Yeshua speaks Psalm 22’s opening, the people around the cross have already enacted and spoken its language. Garments are divided. Heads are shaken. Trust in God is mocked in language closely echoing the psalm.

The psalm is already on the hill before Yeshua names it.

The second problem is the word *forsaken*. One English word is often made to carry several different claims as though they were identical:

- Yeshua suffered.
- Yeshua was not rescued from the cross.
- Yeshua experienced genuine desolation.
- Yeshua bore sin and judgment within his mission.
- The Father repudiated or ceased to love the Son.
- The Father and Son became metaphysically separated.
- The life of God was somehow torn in two.

Those are not one proposition.

The first three are plainly present in the Passion. The fourth belongs to the wider canonical theology of the cross. The final three do not follow automatically from the cry, and the wider canon gives strong reasons to deny actual filial repudiation and metaphysical rupture.

This study will slow the sentence down before doctrine, psychology, or devotion runs away with it.

The question is not whether Yeshua suffered. He did.

The question is what his suffering permits us to conclude about the Father, the Son, and the purpose being carried through the suffering.

Aliyah’s governing practice is:

> **When Scripture is quoted, go home with the quotation.**

## The tool: scriptural self-location

A quotation can do more than decorate speech.

It can identify the kind of moment a speaker is inhabiting.

In the wilderness, Yeshua answers three temptations with three passages from Deuteronomy. Those passages are not random religious slogans. Their home contexts concern Israel in the wilderness: bread, testing, worship, and whether the son will trust YHWH where Israel failed.

Yeshua does not merely find verses that sound relevant. He locates his testing inside Israel’s story.

That is what I mean by **scriptural self-location**:

> **A speaker uses an older text not only to describe a feeling, but to locate the present event inside the older text’s world.**

A modern literary term for this effect is **metalepsis**: one quoted line draws a larger source-text into a new scene.

A Jewish word often used for something similar is **remez**—a hint.

The word needs a warning. The familiar four-level *PaRDeS* scheme—*peshat, remez, derash, sod*—is a later systematization. Matthew 27 does not document Yeshua applying a formally named first-century interpretive rule called *remez*. Claiming that would be history by enthusiasm.

But if *remez* is used descriptively to mean:

> **a deliberate hint that sends the hearer back to a larger passage**

then it names a legitimate question.

Did Yeshua quote only the one line whose language expressed his anguish?

Or did the opening line also identify Psalm 22 as the scriptural world of the event?

The answer will not come from the label. It must come from the text.

## The words Matthew preserved

Matthew writes:

> **Περὶ δὲ τὴν ἐνάτην ὥραν ἀνεβόησεν ὁ Ἰησοῦς φωνῇ μεγάλῃ λέγων· Ἠλί Ἠλί λεμὰ σαβαχθανί; τοῦτ’ ἔστιν· Θεέ μου θεέ μου, ἱνατί με ἐγκατέλιπες;**
>
> *Peri de tēn enatēn hōran aneboēsen ho Iēsous phōnē megalē legōn: “Ēli, Ēli, lema sabachthani?” tout’ estin: “Thee mou, thee mou, hinati me enkatelipes?”*
>
> “About the ninth hour Yeshua cried out with a loud voice, saying, ‘Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?’—that is, ‘My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?’”  
> — Matthew 27:46, working rendering

Matthew preserves the sound of the Semitic cry and then translates it for Greek readers.

The source line in the Hebrew Psalter is:

> **אֵלִי אֵלִי לָמָה עֲזַבְתָּנִי רָחוֹק מִישׁוּעָתִי דִּבְרֵי שַׁאֲגָתִי׃**
>
> *Eli, Eli, lamah azavtani; rachoq mishu‘ati, divrei sha’agati.*
>
> “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? Far from my rescue are the words of my roaring.”  
> — Psalm 22:1 in English numbering; Psalm 22:2 in Hebrew numbering, working rendering

The Hebrew psalm has **azavtani**, from the Hebrew root עזב, “leave” or “forsake.”

The Gospel cry has **sabachthani**, representing an Aramaic form from the root שׁבק, which can carry the same sense.

Matthew’s **Eli** looks closer to the Hebrew form. Mark preserves **Eloi**, a more plainly Aramaic-looking form. The evangelists give us Greek transliterations of a Semitic cry, not an audio recording. The exact phonetic form spoken on the hill cannot be reconstructed with certainty.

That uncertainty costs the argument nothing essential.

Both evangelists translate the cry. Both identify its sense with Psalm 22:1.

This connection is **steel: direct quotation**.

Grammar, however, will not perform the theology for us.

The Hebrew **azavtani** is a qatal form. Matthew’s Greek **enkatelipes** is an aorist. Neither form proves that the abandonment was final, temporary, metaphysical, relational, perceived, judicial, or complete in every possible sense.

The forms present the forsaking as real within the speaker’s cry.

They do not tell us what happened inside the Trinity.

The repeated address also matters:

> **“My God, my God.”**

The complaint is spoken *to* the one being accused of distance. Forsakenness-language and continuing address stand together in the same line.

The text gives us that tension. Interpretation must not erase either side of it.

## Suffering is not the same proposition as repudiation

Let me state this plainly:

> **Yeshua’s suffering does not prove that the Father repudiated him.**

The Passion leaves no room to minimize his suffering. He is betrayed, abandoned by disciples, falsely accused, mocked, struck, scourged, exposed, crucified, and left without the rescue his enemies demand.

Purpose does not erase any of that.

Neither does suffering erase purpose.

Matthew has spent the journey to Jerusalem telling readers that the cross is not an accident overtaking Yeshua before he can complete his work:

- He repeatedly predicts his death and resurrection.
- He says the Son of Man came to give his life as a ransom for many.
- At the meal, he interprets the cup as his covenant blood poured out for many for forgiveness.
- In Gethsemane, he asks whether the cup can pass and then consents: **“Not as I will, but as you.”**

The choice is on the record before the nails.

Knowledge is not anesthesia. Consent is not immunity. A mission can be knowingly and willingly entered while containing an abyss no advance description can make painless.

But the mission means we cannot read the cry as though the Gospel has suddenly forgotten who the speaker is, why he came, or what he repeatedly said would happen.

Christian confession makes the tension sharper, not easier.

Yeshua is fully human, so the agony cannot be theater.

Yeshua is not merely human, so his identity and mission cannot be erased to make the scene psychologically relatable.

The task is not to choose humanity or Christology.

The task is to refuse to diminish either.

## The first temptation returns

Matthew has shown us this test before.

Immediately after the baptismal voice names Yeshua as the beloved Son, the tempter approaches with:

> **“If you are the Son of God…”**  
> — Matthew 4:3, 6

The demand is proof through self-serving power.

Turn stones into bread.

Throw yourself down and force angelic rescue.

Accept the kingdoms by worshiping the wrong ruler.

The devil even quotes Psalm 91 to make presumption sound like faith.

Yeshua answers with Deuteronomy. The home contexts matter:

- Deuteronomy 8:3 concerns Israel’s hunger, manna, and learning that life depends on YHWH’s word.
- Deuteronomy 6:16 recalls Massah, where Israel tested YHWH by demanding proof of His presence.
- Deuteronomy 6:13 commands exclusive worship within the same wilderness instruction.

The Son answers the wilderness test by inhabiting Israel’s Scripture and remaining faithful where Israel failed.

Now listen to Golgotha:

> **“If you are the Son of God, come down from the cross.”**  
> — Matthew 27:40

The challenge formula returns verbatim within Matthew.

Again, sonship is placed under pressure.

Again, rescue is demanded as proof.

Again, Scripture is enlisted in the test.

Again, Yeshua refuses to establish his identity on the adversary’s terms.

The scenes are not identical. In the wilderness, Yeshua rebuts the tempter directly. At the cross, he prays to God. One is counter-speech; the other is lament.

But Matthew’s verbal and narrative linkage is strong:

- “If you are the Son of God.”
- Scripture used as pressure.
- A demand for spectacular rescue.
- The temptation to prove sonship through self-preservation.
- Yeshua’s refusal.
- Yeshua speaking from within Scripture.

This is more than an imaginative thematic comparison.

> **Matthew presents Golgotha as the return and culmination of the Son-of-God test.**

That judgment is **strong intra-Matthean allusion**. Matthew need not interrupt the story with a narrator’s explanation when the distinctive challenge formula itself recurs.

A further claim must be graded separately: that Yeshua consciously selected Psalm 22 specifically in order to answer the returning wilderness temptation. That is plausible within his established scriptural practice, but Matthew does not directly narrate that particular interior connection.

What the narrative clearly shows is that he does not come down.

He does not perform for his accusers.

He remains faithful through the final form of the test.

In the wilderness he answers the adversary horizontally.

At the cross he takes the scriptural language of the test and turns it vertically toward God.

## Read the psalm he named

Go home with the quotation.

Psalm 22 is not a neat progression from sadness to a motivational ending. It is a prayer in which experience, memory, accusation, petition, and praise struggle in one body.

### The abyss

> “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?  
> Why are you far from my rescue, from the words of my roaring?  
> My God, I call by day, and you do not answer;  
> by night, and there is no rest for me.”  
> — Psalm 22:1–2, working rendering

The speaker does not merely say, “I am sad.”

He says God is far.

He says he cries and receives no answer.

The language of forsakenness belongs to the psalm. No responsible Christian reading should bleach it out because the ending becomes brighter.

### The remembered God

> “Yet you are holy, enthroned on Israel’s praises.  
> In you our fathers trusted; they trusted, and you delivered them.”  
> — Psalm 22:3–4

This is not necessarily serene confidence. It can intensify the complaint:

> *You delivered them. Why not me?*

Memory of divine faithfulness may comfort a sufferer. It may also sharpen the pain when the remembered pattern seems absent now.

### The mockers’ script

> “All who see me mock me…  
> ‘He rolled himself onto YHWH—let Him deliver him!  
> Let Him rescue him, since He delights in him!’”  
> — Psalm 22:7–8, working rendering

These are the words Matthew closely echoes in the mouths around the cross.

The psalm gives Yeshua language for the cry and Matthew language for the enemies.

### The body in the dust

> “I am poured out like water…  
> my strength is dried like a potsherd,  
> and my tongue clings to my jaws;

Then:

> “They divide my garments among themselves,  
> and for my clothing they cast lots.”  
> — Psalm 22:18

Verse 16 contains a genuine textual problem: “like a lion, my hands and feet” in the dominant Masoretic tradition versus forms behind ancient witnesses often rendered “they pierced” or “they dug.”

That debate deserves its own excavation. This study does not need to force it. The Passion–Psalm connection is already visible without pretending that an unsettled line is simple.

### The petition

> “But you, YHWH, do not be far away.  
> My strength, hurry to my help.”  
> — Psalm 22:19

The prayer does not treat the opening experience as the only possible account of reality. It asks the God who feels far not to be far.

### The turn

By verse 22, the voice has moved into proclamation:

> “I will declare your name to my brothers;  
> in the midst of the assembly I will praise you.”  
> — Psalm 22:22

Then the psalm gives its own retrospective account:

> **“For He did not despise or detest the affliction of the afflicted one;  
> He did not hide His face from him;

> — Psalm 22:24, working rendering

The psalm beginning with “Why have you forsaken me?” later says:

- He did not despise the afflicted one.
- He did not hide His face from him.
- He heard the cry.

That does not make the opening question dishonest.

It means the opening question is not the psalm’s final account of God.

### The horizon widens

> “All the ends of the earth will remember and return to YHWH,  
> and all the families of the nations will worship before you.  
> For the kingdom belongs to YHWH,

A cry beginning with apparent abandonment ends with an assembly, the nations, the kingdom, and a coming generation hearing what God has done.

Do not use the ending to silence the beginning.

Do not use the beginning to erase the ending.

The psalm insists on both.

## What did Yeshua intend by quoting the opening?

Not every proposed answer has equal support.

The following conclusions are well grounded:

1. The words express the genuine anguish of the moment.
2. Yeshua purposefully identifies himself and the event with Psalm 22’s afflicted speaker.
3. The quotation places the Passion within Psalm 22’s scriptural world.
4. Because the scene has already activated other parts of the psalm, readers have strong reason to retrieve more than its first verse.

The remaining uncertainty concerns scope, not whether the quotation is purposeful.

The text does not let us determine exhaustively:

- whether every historical hearer recognized the source;
- whether Yeshua intended every hearer to retrieve every verse;
- whether he consciously reviewed the psalm’s entire movement at that instant;
- how immediate agony, lifelong formation, and deliberate selection interacted in his human consciousness.

Those are questions about reception and interior mechanism.

They are not reasons to retreat from intentional invocation.

The Gospel portrait supplies established character. Yeshua repeatedly interprets his mission through Scripture, uses context-bearing passages in decisive tests, predicts his Passion, consents to the cup, and prays Scripture while dying.

The scene supplies context. Psalm 22 has already been activated through garments, head-shaking, and the trust-taunt.

The cry supplies conduct. Yeshua speaks the psalm’s distinctive opening aloud.

Together, this warrants the conclusion:

> **Yeshua deliberately invokes Psalm 22 as the scriptural frame of his suffering.**

The stronger paraphrase—

> *Retrieve the entire psalm and consciously activate every movement of it*—

remains a compelling reconstruction of how the invocation may function. It is not directly narrated.

Christian Scripture later strengthens the whole-psalm reading. Hebrews writes:

> “The one sanctifying and those being sanctified are all from One; for this reason he is not ashamed to call them brothers, saying:
>
> **‘I will proclaim your name to my brothers;  
> in the midst of the assembly I will sing your praise.’**”  
> — Hebrews 2:11–12, quoting Psalm 22:22

This is **steel within the Christian canon: direct quotation with explicit Christological attribution**.

Hebrews places Psalm 22’s praising speaker in the Son’s mouth.

That does not prove what every person at Golgotha understood.

It does prove that the New Testament does not confine the psalm’s Christological relevance to verse 1.

The grades are therefore:

### Direct quotation — steel

Yeshua’s cry is Psalm 22:1.

### Passion-scene saturation — strong allusion

Garments, head-shaking, trust-taunt, and cry locate the crucifixion within the psalm’s language-world.

### Purposeful Psalm 22 invocation — strong canonical judgment

Yeshua intentionally identifies the event with Psalm 22. This conclusion rests on the testified act, its immediate context, and his established scriptural practice.

### Exhaustive whole-psalm cue — strong literary/canonical inference

The opening plausibly invites retrieval of the whole prayer, especially because the scene has already activated several of its lines and Hebrews later applies verse 22 to the Son.

### “Recite every line in full” — theological reconstruction

Beautiful and plausible, but not directly stated.

The difference between these grades does not weaken the argument.

It keeps each claim accountable to its evidence.

## Chosen or spontaneous is the wrong fight

We often imagine two possibilities:

Either Yeshua calmly selected Psalm 22 after detached internal analysis,

or agony overwhelmed him and familiar words escaped without intention.

That is a false choice.

A person formed by Scripture can speak reflexively and intentionally at once.

What surfaces under pressure may be precisely what has been chosen through years of formation.

The Gospels present Yeshua’s speech as saturated with Scripture in public controversy, private teaching, temptation, prayer, and death. We do not need the unverifiable claim that he had “prayed this psalm every day since childhood.”

The text gives us enough:

> **Scripture is not an emergency substitute for Yeshua’s own words. Scripture is part of how he understands and performs his mission.**

The cry can erupt from genuine agony.

The cry can be an intentional scriptural act.

It can be both because immediate speech does not cease to be meaningful merely because it comes from the depths of suffering.

Formation is intentionality with a longer fuse.

## The other dying words

Matthew and Mark are not the only Gospel witnesses to Yeshua’s speech from the cross. Each Gospel shapes the Passion with its own emphases, and they should not be flattened into one featureless transcript.

A Christian canonical reading may nevertheless compare them.

### Luke: another psalm

> “Father, into your hands I entrust my spirit.”  
> — Luke 23:46, echoing Psalm 31:5

Another dying word is scriptural prayer.

It is also addressed to the Father in trust.

### John: Scripture named as purpose

> “After this, Yeshua, knowing that everything had now been accomplished, so that the Scripture might be fulfilled, said, ‘I thirst.’”  
> — John 19:28, working rendering

John explicitly presents scriptural fulfillment as a category for speech from the cross.

That does not prove every detail of Matthew’s presentation.

It shows that the Gospel tradition does not treat genuine bodily suffering and purposeful scriptural speech as competitors.

“I thirst” can be bodily truth and scriptural act.

So can Psalm 22.

### Gethsemane: the choice upstream

Matthew establishes willing obedience before the crucifixion:

> “My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me. Yet not as I will, but as you.”  
> — Matthew 26:39

The mission is not invented at the ninth hour.

The ninth hour is where the mission is endured.

### One precision note

Matthew 27:46 is not literally Yeshua’s final breath. Matthew 27:50 records another loud cry before he gives up his spirit, though Matthew does not preserve its words.

The weight of “Eli, Eli” rests on its specificity, placement, and scriptural home—not on the inaccurate dramatic claim that he had one breath left and spent it on this sentence.

## Five different claims hiding inside “forsaken”

Return to the question:

> **Did the Father abandon the Son?**

First ask what *abandon* means.

### 1. The Father did not rescue Yeshua from the cross

Yes.

The mockers demand rescue. None comes. The cup is not removed. Yeshua remains exposed to enemies and death.

This is plain narrative fact.

### 2. Yeshua entered genuine desolation

Yes—if desolation means the human extremity voiced by Psalm 22:1.

The cry is not theater. Its first line is not disposable packaging around a triumphant ending. Even if the whole psalm is active, Yeshua reaches its destination through the abyss, not around it.

The Gospel gives us his words, not a clinical inventory of every proposition in his human consciousness. That boundary should be honored without reducing the cry to an accidental verbal reflex.

### 3. Yeshua bore judgment and sin within his mission

Yes, as a Christian canonical claim.

Matthew says his life is a ransom for many and his blood is poured out for forgiveness. The cup and darkness carry judgment associations. The wider New Testament speaks of curse, sin-bearing, sacrifice, and reconciliation.

But none of this is encoded mechanically in the tense of one verb.

Atonement belongs in the canonical argument.

It cannot be smuggled in as grammar.

### 4. The Father repudiated the Son or ceased to love him

No.

The cry by itself does not answer every question about experienced or judicial forsakenness, but the wider canonical evidence does not leave actual filial rejection equally open.

Psalm 22’s destination says God did not despise the afflicted one, did not hide His face, and heard the cry.

Yeshua continues to address God as **“my God.”**

The mission narratives present Father and Son as united in purpose. In John, Yeshua says the disciples will leave him alone:

> “Yet I am not alone, because the Father is with me.”  
> — John 16:32

Luke’s dying prayer addresses **Father** and entrusts his spirit into the Father’s hands.

The resurrection vindicates the Son and his mission. The New Testament presents the cross as God’s reconciling action, not as the Father ceasing to love the Son.

Christians may dispute how to define judicial forsakenness, withdrawal of consolation, curse-bearing, or the Son’s experience of dereliction.

Actual filial repudiation is not simply “unproven by one verse.” It is inconsistent with the canonical pattern.

### 5. The Father and Son were metaphysically separated

No.

The text does not say this, and classical Christian confession cannot speak as though the divine life fractured, the Trinity temporarily became two, or the Father ceased to be Father toward the eternal Son.

Judicial judgment is not a synonym for ontological rupture.

Experienced dereliction is not a synonym for the dissolution of divine unity.

Nonrescue is not a synonym for the Father’s absence from the reconciling work.

One English word has been carrying too many suitcases.

Set them down and open them separately.

## Did Yeshua believe the Father was gone?

No text gives uncomplicated access to every proposition in Yeshua’s human consciousness.

That is a real boundary, but it does not make every proposed reading equally plausible.

Matthew does not write:

> “At that moment Yeshua concluded that the Father had ceased to be present or to love him.”

Nor does Matthew write:

> “Yeshua felt no abandonment and spoke only to teach the crowd.”

Matthew gives us something more demanding:

- Yeshua genuinely cries the language of forsakenness.
- He continues to say **“my God”** twice.
- He deliberately invokes the opening of Psalm 22.
- The Passion has already activated that psalm.
- The psalm itself moves from unanswered distance to the declaration that God heard.
- Yeshua knowingly embraced the mission before the cross.
- The wider canonical witness denies that the Father ceased to love or finally repudiated the Son.

The responsible conclusion is neither:

> “Yeshua mistakenly believed the Father was no longer his Father,”

nor:

> “Yeshua was merely signaling victory and did not mean the question.”

It is:

> **Yeshua voices genuine dereliction while deliberately placing the event inside a prayer whose destination is hearing, praise, and the reign of God.**

The abyss is real.

It is not the whole psalm.

It is not the canonical verdict on the relation of the Father and Son.

---

## The doctrine people often import

A common Christian explanation says:

> “God is too holy to look at sin, so when Yeshua bore sin, the Father turned His face away.”

The sentence may be intended as a metaphor for judgment.

It is not what Matthew says.

Habakkuk 1:13 is often used to support “God cannot look at sin,” but that verse belongs to a complaint asking why God *does* look on treachery without immediately acting. It is not a metaphysical claim that God cannot perceive evil.

Psalm 22:24 says, in the prayer Yeshua invokes:

> **“He did not hide His face from him.”**

That does not settle every theory of atonement.

It does mean the “turned face” slogan cannot be presented as though it were Psalm 22’s own explanation.

The cross judges sin.

The Son bears what he came to bear.

The Father does not remove the cup.

But Christian theology should describe the cross as God’s act of reconciliation, not as the Father becoming the Son’s enemy or ceasing to love him.

Paul says:

> “God was in Christ reconciling the world to Himself.”  
> — 2 Corinthians 5:19, common rendering of the sense

Hebrews says Christ offered himself **through the eternal Spirit** to God (Hebrews 9:14).

The cross is not a fracture in God’s life.

It is the terrible, unified act by which God enters and overcomes the alienation of sin.

That is theological synthesis stated as Christian conviction, not smuggled into the definition of an Aramaic verb.

---

## Remez and the invitation to retrieve

The mockers have already spoken Psalm 22’s language.

Yeshua gives its opening.

Could the cry mean, in effect:

> *You are standing inside this psalm. Here is its beginning. Retrieve the prayer.*

Yes. That is a compelling reading.

It explains why this opening matters in a scene crowded with the psalm.

It fits Yeshua’s established use of context-bearing Scripture.

It fits the Gospel tradition’s presentation of scriptural speech at death.

It fits Hebrews’ attribution of Psalm 22:22 to the Son.

It also explains the line’s enduring interpretive force: the cry does not end thought; it opens a text.

But the grades must remain visible.

### What is steel

- Yeshua quotes Psalm 22:1.
- Matthew’s Passion contains several clear Psalm 22 correspondences.
- Hebrews directly places Psalm 22:22 in the Son’s mouth.

### What is strong allusion

- Matthew 27:43 closely echoes Psalm 22:8.
- Garments, head-shaking, trust-taunt, and cry jointly locate the scene in Psalm 22.
- Matthew’s wilderness and cross scenes form a repeated Son-of-God testing pattern.

### What is strong canonical judgment

- Yeshua purposefully invokes Psalm 22.
- The quotation identifies Psalm 22 as part of the event’s interpretive frame.
- Yeshua’s characteristic scriptural use depends upon source context rather than isolated slogans.

### What is strong inference

- The opening invites readers to retrieve the psalm’s larger movement.
- Yeshua intends his hearers to recognize more than the lexical content of one verse.
- The wilderness reprise helps explain why self-rescue would contradict faithful sonship.

### What remains theological reconstruction

- Yeshua consciously commands every hearer to recite every verse in full.
- Every movement of the psalm was simultaneously explicit in his human consciousness.
- Each Passion detail is a one-to-one predictive code written solely for the crucifixion.

The reconstruction may be right without becoming direct textual fact.

The best argument does not need a false badge.

---

## The answer hidden in the refusal to come down

The crowd thinks rescue would prove sonship.

Matthew has already taught readers the opposite.

In the wilderness, throwing himself down would not have proved trust. It would have been testing God.

At the cross, coming down would not have proved his mission. It would have abandoned it.

The taunt says:

> “If you are the Son of God, save yourself.”

The Gospel’s answer is:

> **Because he is the Son, he does not save himself from the work by which he saves others.**

Matthew supplies strong narrative support:

> “He saved others; he cannot save himself.”  
> — Matthew 27:42

The mockers intend a contradiction.

The narrative creates irony.

The inability is not lack of power. It is the moral necessity of love, obedience, and mission. He cannot save himself *and* complete the work in the way he has chosen to complete it.

The mockers speak Psalm 22’s demand for rescue.

Yeshua gives Psalm 22’s cry.

Not because purpose has vanished.

Because purpose has reached the place where obedience hurts.

Purpose does not erase suffering.

Suffering does not erase purpose.

The cross holds both until neither can be reduced to the other.

---

## Where the text aims

An outsider need not accept Christian theology to see the literary architecture.

A condemned man hangs in a scene narrated through an ancient psalm. His enemies speak that psalm’s language against him. He then gives its opening line aloud. The Gospel has previously shown him answering an “if you are the Son” test through context-bearing Scripture, and the same distinctive test returns at his death.

This is more than random verbal debris.

It is a serious ancient claim about how the death should be read.

For believers, the warning cuts in two directions.

Do not protect Christology by making the suffering theatrical.

Do not protect the suffering by stripping the speaker of identity and mission.

The appalling reality is not that Yeshua forgot the mission.

It is that the mission went through this.

His life is not only a rescue accomplished for us. It is a revelation of how truth is inhabited under pressure. In the wilderness, controversy, prayer, and death, Scripture is not ammunition detached from context. It is the world in which he recognizes and performs faithfulness.

The lesson is not merely:

> “When your own words run out, borrow a verse.”

Maybe his words did not run out.

Maybe these were exactly the words.

A better practice is:

> **Let Scripture form you before the darkness, so that when pressure strips away performance, what remains is language deep enough to hold both the wound and the destination.**

One caution remains.

Do not use Yeshua’s mission to tell every suffering person that every wound was specifically assigned as necessary cargo.

The text tells us what his cross means.

It does not authorize us to explain every person’s trauma from above.

What it gives sufferers is a prayer large enough to say:

> “Why have you forsaken me?”

without requiring that the experience become the final truth about God.

## The shovel: how to read a cry that quotes Scripture

This study’s tool is **context recovery under pressure**.

### Step one: identify what is explicit

What words are quoted?

Is the source identifiable?

Is there a citation formula?

Here, the source is Psalm 22:1. Steel.

### Step two: separate the cry from conclusions built on it

Write the propositions separately:

- suffering,
- nonrescue,
- experienced desolation,
- sin-bearing and judgment,
- filial repudiation,
- metaphysical separation.

Do not let one word prove all six.

### Step three: go home with the quotation

Read the source passage.

What does it say before and after the quoted line?

What tension does the source itself preserve?

Psalm 22 moves from forsakenness-language to the declaration that God did not hide His face and heard.

### Step four: inspect the new scene for other traces

Look for repeated words, images, gestures, and arguments.

Here: garments, head-shaking, trust-taunt, cry.

### Step five: compare the speaker’s established use of Scripture

Does this speaker use isolated slogans or context-bearing passages?

Yeshua’s wilderness citations draw their force from Deuteronomy’s larger wilderness setting.

Established character and repeated conduct are evidence of purposeful action. Recognizing intention does not require a transcript of private consciousness.

### Step six: distinguish testimony, literary presentation, and interior mechanism

What does the evangelist testify happened?

How does the evangelist arrange and interpret it?

What can and cannot be said about the speaker’s unreported psychological process?

These are distinguishable questions, but they should not be separated as though literary presentation cancels testimony about the event.

### Step seven: grade the metal

- **Direct quotation**
- **Strong allusion**
- **Shared motif**
- **Literary synthesis**
- **Canonical judgment**
- **Canonical theological synthesis**
- **Doctrinal interpretation**
- **Speculation**

Do not invent a flattering tier because you love the conclusion.

Do not downgrade established textual linkage merely because the narrator does not stop to explain it.

### Step eight: audit salience

Before asking, “Is this defensible?” ask:

> **Why are we making this the controlling statement?**

A true statement can distort a short reading if it pushes equally necessary truths out of view.

That is how a card about genuine suffering becomes a doctrine of metaphysical abandonment without noticing the border it crossed.

## The chain, standing back

- **Psalm 22:1** — the direct source of the cry.
- **Psalm 22:7–8, 18** — mockery, head-shaking, trust-taunt, and divided garments.
- **Matthew 27:35, 39, 43, 46** — the Passion testified and narrated within the psalm’s language.
- **Matthew 4:3–11** — the earlier “if you are the Son” Scripture-test.
- **Matthew 27:40** — the distinctive challenge returns with a demand for self-rescue.
- **Matthew 26:36–46** — the cup faced and the mission willingly embraced before Golgotha.
- **Luke 23:46 / Psalm 31:5** — another dying prayer spoken from Scripture and entrusted to the Father.
- **John 16:32** — the Son’s declaration that the Father is with him.
- **John 19:28** — a Gospel explicitly linking speech from the cross with scriptural fulfillment.
- **Psalm 22:22 / Hebrews 2:12** — the afflicted psalmist’s praise placed directly in the Son’s mouth.
- **Psalm 22:24** — the declaration that God did not hide His face and heard.
- **Psalm 22:27–28** — the ends of the earth, the nations, and YHWH’s kingdom.
- **The resurrection** — the canonical vindication of the Son and his mission.

The cry is genuine lament and purposeful scriptural action.

Yeshua deliberately identifies his Passion with Psalm 22.

The scene’s saturation and the canon’s later use give strong reason to read beyond the opening, while the precise claim that every hearer was commanded to retrieve every verse remains an inference.

Read what happens through the prayer he named.

Do not skip its abyss.

Do not stop before its destination.

## A prayer

Father,

Keep me from making the cross easier than it was.

Do not let me use purpose to numb the suffering, or suffering to erase the purpose.

Teach me to hear Yeshua’s cry without forcing one word to say more than it says. When he says, “Why have you forsaken me?” keep me from turning anguish into theater—and keep me from turning anguish into a fracture in You.

Let me go home with the quotation.

Let me hear the mockers speaking the language of a psalm whose irony they cannot control. Let me see the Son refusing, again, to prove himself by self-rescue. Let me read until the prayer beginning with distance reaches the declaration: You did not hide Your face. You heard.

Form Your Word in me before the dark.

Not as a shelf of slogans, but as truth deep enough to rise when everything shallow has been stripped away.

When I cannot reconcile the wound with the destination, give me the honesty to say “why,” the relationship to keep saying “my God,” and the patience not to make my present experience the final definition of who You are.

For the glory of the Father, through Yeshua the Son, by the Spirit through whom he offered himself.

Amen.

## Research notes for council review

### Publication posture

This is a Christian module, not a neutralized comparative-religion essay. Its governing commitments are:

- Yeshua is fully human and not merely human.
- His suffering is genuine.
- His mission and identity remain interpretively relevant at the cross.
- Linguistic and historical evidence may not be distorted to protect doctrine.
- Theology must be labeled as theology rather than smuggled in as grammar.
- Jewish Scripture must first be represented honestly within its own literary world.
- Non-Christian readers are invited to examine the argument without pretending they already share its premises.

### Claims that should survive review

1. **Matthew 27:46 and Mark 15:34 directly quote Psalm 22:1 in translated sense.**
2. **Matthew 27 contains multiple strong Psalm 22 correspondences before the cry.**
3. **Matthew 27:43 closely alludes to Psalm 22:8; it is not a formally marked direct quotation.**
4. **The text establishes Matthean irony but does not narrate the mockers’ subjective awareness of the allusion.**
5. **Matthew 4 and 27 repeat the distinctive “if you are the Son of God” challenge and pressure toward demonstrative rescue.**
6. **The wilderness–cross relationship is strong intra-Matthean allusion; Yeshua’s precise interior coordination of the two scenes remains inferential.**
7. **Yeshua’s Deuteronomy citations in Matthew 4 draw their force from Israel’s wilderness context.**
8. **The Gospel portrait and immediate context support purposeful invocation of Psalm 22 by Yeshua.**
9. **The exhaustive scope of the intended whole-psalm retrieval remains a strong literary/canonical inference.**
10. **Hebrews 2:12 directly attributes Psalm 22:22 to the Son.**
11. **Psalm 22:24 says God did not hide His face from the afflicted one and heard the cry.**
12. **The wider canon contradicts actual filial repudiation while leaving room for debate about judicial forsakenness and experienced dereliction.**
13. **John 19:28 presents scriptural fulfillment as part of its framing of speech from the cross.**
14. **Matthew 27:46 is not literally Yeshua’s final breath; Matthew 27:50 records another loud cry without preserving its words.**

### Claims that must remain graded

- **Yeshua purposefully invokes Psalm 22:** strong canonical judgment grounded in testimony, context, and established character.
- **The opening cues retrieval of the whole psalm:** strong literary/canonical inference.
- **The cry means “recite every verse in full”:** theological reconstruction.
- **Matthew presents the cross as the return of the wilderness Son-of-God test:** strong intra-Matthean allusion.
- **Yeshua consciously selected Psalm 22 specifically to answer that returning test:** plausible inference.
- **The precise contents and mechanism of Yeshua’s subjective experience:** not directly narrated.
- **Judicial forsakenness as an atonement category:** Christian doctrinal interpretation from the wider canon.
- **Actual cessation of the Father’s love or repudiation of the Son:** contradicted by the canonical evidence.
- **Relational or metaphysical rupture in the Trinity:** not established by the text and outside classical Christian bounds if understood as fracture in the divine being.

### Historical-language cautions

- Matthew’s *Eli* and Mark’s *Eloi* differ; the exact historical phonetic form cannot be reconstructed simply from either Greek transliteration.
- *Sabachthani* reflects an Aramaic root; Psalm 22’s Hebrew has *azavtani*.
- Do not use the Hebrew qatal or Greek aorist to prove finality, duration, or metaphysical separation.
- Use *remez* descriptively, not as proof that a later formal PaRDeS system governed the event.
- Do not call Matthew 27:46 Yeshua’s “last breath.”
- Do not claim Psalm 22:31, “he has done it,” is a direct quotation behind John’s “It is finished” without additional evidence.
- Do not build the module on the disputed “pierced / like a lion” reading of Psalm 22:16.

### Suggested scholarly checks

- Richard B. Hays, *Echoes of Scripture in the Gospels* — metalepsis and Gospel scriptural echoes.
- Jason Robert Combs, “An Extended Inverted Allusion to Psalm 22 in Mark 15” — the reverse-order Psalm 22 proposal in Mark’s Passion.
- Daniel M. Gurtner, *The Torn Veil: Matthew’s Exposition of the Death of Jesus* — Matthew’s Passion theology and literary structure.
- S. van Tilborg, “Language, Meaning, Sense and Reference: Matthew’s Passion Narrative and Psalm 22” — Psalm 22 as an anchor for Matthew’s Passion narration.
- Henk Jan de Jonge, “The Reading Asabthani in Luther’s Translation of Matthew 27.46” — Hebrew and Aramaic forms behind the cry.
- Bryan R. Dyer, “‘In the Midst of the Assembly I Will Praise You’: Hebrews 2.12 and Its Contribution to the Argument of the Epistle” — Psalm 22:22 in Hebrews.
- Jewish reception of Psalm 22 should be checked independently so the module does not treat Christian canonical reuse as the psalm’s only legitimate reading.
